# Hatching

The smallest of the four would not eat from the dish. So she fed it from her mouth. She had told herself, before they hatched, that she would not do it for this clutch, because feeding from the mouth bound you in the body, below decision, and she had wanted to keep one part of herself unbound.

It took the warm pulp with the blind greed of a thing four days in the world, not yet able to imagine a world that might not provide. Its down was wet at the crown where the egg-water had not dried. In the river-light through the seaward apertures it threw back a register she had no word for, a hue above the hue the others would call the color of spring. *New, and wet, and alive.* She held the small body against her and let her throat go grey over it. Only the hatchling could read her, and it did not know yet that grey meant anything.

There were four. She counted them the way he counted, the side of one finger laid along each small spine. They were no longer shells but persons, loud, turning their blind-bright heads toward any motion, four small honest bodies that could not yet hold a color and broadcast everything into the light. The counting did not steady her. He did not have it in him to say *the count has stopped working.* He had it in him only to count again.

◆

She kept the record because it was the only motion she had that did not cost her hope she could not afford. She set down the morning of the hatching in the keeping-book that was hers and not the Reckoning's, the book the rock did not keep. She set down that there had been four, and that all four had come out of the shell whole, and that the air carried the thing in the water, and now in the milt of the few fish that still ran the shoal—the thing she had measured season on season, the thing the Concord had studied and not stopped. She did not write that it was cruel. Cruelty belonged to her, not to the marsh, and the record was the marsh's. She wrote the count, and the morning, and the air, and let whatever read it draw the line.

Then she went out to the south hide before the others woke. The river came up out of the dark in its slow flood and the light broke on it into the moving grille that had crossed this stretch since before her line kept the marsh. Nothing called. The criers were eleven flocks and then four. Along the south shoal a single river-crier stood in the reeds with its crest down and its neck folded, and fished the empty water with the faith of a thing that did not know it was the last of its stretch, and caught nothing, and fished again. The patience of it was the most terrible thing she had seen since the spring-caller signed his last unanswered dawn.

She thought of the smallest hatchling taking the pulp with the same faith. The old fisher's faith and the new mouth's were the same faith, and one was already wrong, and the other did not know yet, and she was the only creature on the shoal who could hold both and could do nothing with the holding but keep it.

She did not write the crier down. It was hers that morning. To set it beside the count would have made it the marsh's, and she wanted, for once, one true thing only her own.

◆

Iren did not come home the first two nights. On the third he came and did not sleep. On the fourth she woke in the dark of the warm passage to the sound of him counting.

He was asleep. The floor tilted under her where her people felt the truth of such things. He lay with his crest flat and his throat held even in sleep, the discipline others read as cold and she alone read as grief under pressure. His finger moved against the sand, the old motion, the count, and his mouth made the shapes without sound. Not the count of four. It did not come out to four, did not come out to anything. It started over. It went on.

She did not wake him. There is a mercy that is also a cowardice, and she could not tell which she practiced.

In the morning his throat told her before his mouth could. At his throat the grey had gone past holding, to a shade she had seen perhaps five times in their whole bond. On every one of them something had ended. She did not ask how bad. The body had told her. She did not say bring it to the Reckoning, because she had read on his throat that the Reckoning was no longer a place a thing like this could be brought.

"You count in your sleep," she said.

He went very still, the stillness of a man seen doing the thing he most needs not to be seen doing. "What do I count?"

"Not the four. It doesn't come out even. It starts over." She put the back of one hand against his nape. "What are you counting, Iren, that doesn't come out even?"

When he spoke it was not to the question.

"There's a thing I built that shows its strata. Every layer. I made it show what rests on what, because once a foreman came to me with his throat grey and no layer to point at, and I filed him as caution and signed the release, and the bay failed in the third hour, in a lower layer I had not checked. So I built a thing that could never do that." His finger moved once and stopped. "And it shows me the strata, Liss. Every layer holds. Each rests honestly on the one beneath. And the strata lead somewhere I would have died to prevent, and there is no layer to lift, because the wrongness isn't in any layer. It's in the line they make going down. I taught the whole Reckoning to ask *show us the layer*, and now I am the foreman with the grey throat and nothing to point at, and there is no one above me to file me as caution."

"Is it the marsh," she said. "Is it the thing in the water." Because if it was, there was a sentence for it.

"No," he said, and the *no* was the worst thing he had ever said to her, because it closed every door she knew how to open. "It's larger than the marsh. I can't make it a session. I can't make it a study. I can't bring it to anyone, because the thing it would be brought to is the thing that—" He stopped. "I count in my sleep because in the day there is nothing that comes out even. I count the four because the four still come out to four. It's the only count left that closes."

◆

She took him to the south hide, which she had never done, because the hide was hers the way the deep hall was his. The crier was there. It fished and caught nothing and fished again, and she watched his eye stop reading it as a system and start reading it as a body.

"That's the last of this stretch," she said. "I don't write it down. I write the count, when it's none. He's mine. I let myself keep one. The way you keep one wall."

Iren said nothing for a long time. The crier fished. The light came up off the river and nothing called.

"I keep the four," he said at last, very low, his throat grey in the open on the rail, where anyone on the river could read him, which she had never seen him allow. "I tell myself it's a wall. It isn't. It's a count. It's your record. The same act. You write the crier so the marsh signed its dawn in a hand and not a number. I count the four so that—" and here he let the sentence go all the way to its end, the first end she had ever heard him reach— "so that when I can't stop it, there will have been four, and someone will have known their down in the light, and fed the smallest from her mouth, and it will have been *true* that they were here, even though there is no after in which the truth does anyone good. It isn't for after. There isn't going to be an after. It's the fidelity. It doesn't need the after to be worth doing." He turned his grey throat from the river. "A wall is just a record you can stand inside for a while."

She did not answer, because the only true answer was that he was right. The marsh would die and the four would hatch into its dying and neither his building nor her keeping would save them. But the thing they had never been able to give each other had, for one morning on the shoal, been given: that they were doing the same act from opposite ends, and the act had a name now, and the name was fidelity.

The crier fished the empty water and caught nothing and fished again, and they stood and let it, and did not write it down, and did not stop it.

◆

She fed the smallest from her mouth again that night, because it still would not take the dish, and its down had dried at the crown and threw back the register above the hue with a strength it had not had at hatching. *New, and dry, and alive.* She counted the four with the side of one finger, and the count came out to four, and she let her throat go grey over them in the dark where only the four could read her. She stayed long after she knew the count, her mate counting beside her, awake this time, his finger on the four that closed and not the count in his sleep that did not. They kept the same record from opposite ends. Neither said the word for it. They did it anyway, while the four slept and did not know yet that they had hatched into a world that owed them an apology no one living could pay.
