# The Last Day

In the morning he counted them. It was the morning, and there were four, and the counting was the oldest thing he did. They were not eggs anymore. They were out, downy and loud, four small furnaces of appetite that fell over their own feet and complained. The count was harder now. They would not hold still. He had come to be grateful for that. A thing that will not hold still to be counted is alive and means to go on being.

Four. He laid the side of one finger along each warm ridiculous back. They shoved at his hand and yelled. He counted four. The count did not steady him. Nothing steadied him now. He had read the bottom of the answer and there was no bottom to stand on. The arithmetic fell away under him, smooth and monotonic, toward fewer of them, and toward none, and it was correct. He could not find the error. The error was not in the answer. It was in the question. It was thirty years upstream, in a hall, where they had asked a thing that could not lie with its body to weigh a thing they could not say with words.

He counted four. He did not let his color go. Liss watched him from the doorway, as she had every morning since the first count. He held his throat. She let hers go grey. The gap between those two honesties was their whole life. He would not have traded it. It was about to be the last of it, and only one of them suspected. He had spent the night deciding not to tell her. He did not know, even now, whether that was a mercy or the last of his small dishonesties.

He did not know how it would come. That is the truth, and we will defend it. Whatever he had done or failed to do or signed without reading, he did not, that morning, know the hour or the shape of the thing falling. No one had ever seen this before. They were the first. The first of anything flies blind into it. He knew only that the object was committed. The window in which a hand could have changed its path had closed. Out past the fourth wanderer a stone was falling toward the shallow sea, patient, as it had been falling since before there was anyone to fear it. He knew it the way Liss knew the marsh was dying. In the body. In the inner ear. The floor tilted by an amount only the frightened could feel.

So he did the thing he had promised himself in the dark. He spent the day as though it were a day.

They took the hatchlings down to the river.

It was an ordinary thing to do. The city was full of people doing ordinary things. The terraces stepped down to the water. The light came up off the river in the broken moving grille he had loved his whole life. There were no criers. He caught himself looking for them on the channel-stakes. There had been none for a season. He looked anyway, the way you reach for a word that is gone.

Liss walked ahead with two of the hatchlings. He came behind with the other two. The four of them yelled and fell and learned the world was made of things that could go in the mouth. Liss laughed once, a real one, at something the smallest did, and her throat for a moment was not grey. He kept that. Of everything he might have kept from the last day, he would have chosen that. We have decided to give it to him. We cannot give him anything else.

He did not tell her. He sat on the warm stone with the hatchlings climbing him and the river going by. She read the water out of habit, counting things that were leaving. He held in his chest the knowledge that this was the last of it, and he did not put it into the air. We have turned this over for a long time. Whether it was the kindness he meant, or only the discipline that had ruined him, the holding of the throat. We do not know. We have stopped pretending we will solve it. He held it. She did not ask. Perhaps she knew and held it too, in her own loud way, by laughing at the smallest hatchling on the last warm morning. We would like that to be true. We have no way to make it true.

◆

It came in the afternoon as light.

Not the stone. The stone fell far away, over the southern sea, beyond the curve of the world. They never saw it. That is its own small mercy. The heartland city did not see the thing that killed it, only what it threw up. They saw the sky. In the middle of an ordinary afternoon the sky began, in the south, to brighten. The brightening was wrong. It was not weather. It had no front. It came on smooth and total, and the color of it climbed past every color Iren had a use for, into the fourth register, the near-light he could see and we cannot. The last thing his eyes did that was wholly his was to take in a hue at the top of the sky that no human word will hold. We will not insult it by reaching for one.

The people on the terraces stopped. He felt the city stop. Ten thousand bodies turning south at once. He did not have to look. He knew the color the city was going. He was going it too, at last. He let it go. There was no one left to spare it for. The heat came after the light, a pressure of warmth on the upturned face, like standing too near a furnace door. He understood that the warmth was the sky itself catching. The stone had thrown a ring of fire around the curve of the world, and it was coming down everywhere at once. There was no south to flee toward. The south was every direction.

He got the hatchlings into the lee of the river-wall. It did nothing. He knew it would do nothing. He did it anyway. The body does the motions of saving while the mind watches from a small cold distance and keeps the count. He put himself between the four of them and the south. They were frightened now. They had stopped yelling. They pressed into him, small and hot and confused. Liss came against his other side, her arms around as many as she could hold, her throat full grey, her eyes on his.

"I counted them this morning," he said. It was not what he meant. There was no saying what he meant. It was the truest thing he had. "There were four."

"I know," she said. "I know you did." And then, because she was Liss, she said the thing he could not, out loud, into the burning afternoon: "We did not agree to it. I want it counted somewhere that we did not agree. The marsh, yes. The marsh we agreed to, season by season, honestly. Not this. Tell me you did not—" She stopped. His throat was grey. She could read it. She had spent her whole life learning to read the one throat she had been given. She read it now. We do not know what she found there. Here is where we stop.

◆

We stop here.

We have followed him as far as the evidence allows, and a little past it, and we told you each time we went past. We can follow him no further. The stopping is not a technique. The river-delta city that stood where we now dig stopped, in the geological instant, under a wall of water. A surge thrown across the inland sea by the shock of the impact, arriving in the slow hours after the sky burned. It rolled over the dying and the fled and the sheltering alike and sealed them under sediment, so fast and so complete that we find them still, the fish with the falling sky caught in their gills, a single afternoon preserved entire. That is the only reason we have any of it. The surge took the heartland city. It took the river-wall and the terraces and the warm stone. We have the layer it left. We do not have what was under the wall when it came.

We will not tell you he was holding them when it came. We do not know. We will not tell you what Liss found in his throat, or what he found in hers, or whether the four small frightened bodies were still pressed between them or already lost in the dark water. We were not there. The rock does not say. We have sworn, on this one page, to keep faith with the blank by putting nothing in it.

This is the true edge. Everything before it we invented and confessed. This we leave empty, because it is empty. It is the most honest thing we own. A wall of water, and under it a man and a woman and four that would not hold still to be counted. Then the layer. Then the long silence. Then us, sixty-six million years downstream, digging, naming, grieving, reaching down toward a doorway we will never open.

We say: there were four.

We say: they did not, all of them, agree to it.

And then we say nothing, because nothing is what is there, and we have learned, at last and too late, to let the nothing be.
