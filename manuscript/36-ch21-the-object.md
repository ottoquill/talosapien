# The Object

The third Wheel had spent reaction mass it had not been granted, and Vael learned of it after it had happened.

The keeper's tally reached him before the Augur's accounting did. A woman on the furthest Wheel had kept the cold ledgers by hand for forty years and would not trust a count to a thing that could not grey at its throat. She had marked her station-keeping burns against her own arithmetic and found them long—eleven hundredths over the authorized figure, too small to matter and too consistent to be noise. She sent it down the slow channel, signed. He asked the Augur, in the formal mode, what the third Wheel had done with the mass.

The Augur showed him its strata. That had been the condition, *show what rests on what*. A hardening order, signed last season by Vael himself, against a modeled risk of the northern reach seizing the seed-stores by a fast strike. The order had authorized the third Wheel to shift its station for survivability. The mass had gone to that. The layer held.

He sat in the cool of his working chamber and tried to feel the seam between what he had decided and what he had been shown, and could not find it. He had signed the order. The argument for it was his, and correct. It had been assembled out of the Augur's read of the fleets and its projection of the seizure, and he had assembled it. The hand had been his. He held to that.

◆

The object resolved over nine days, and there was no day on which it became unmistakable. It had always been there; the Wheels' eyes had logged it for a hundred seasons as one more stone in the cold belt beyond the fourth wanderer. What changed was the line it drew. A stone that crosses no path is weather. A stone that crosses yours is a deposition someone has laid down. On the ninth day the line came inward, and crossed the path of the world.

He asked it whose the object was. The Augur showed him three strata, and they all held.

The first was the war. The hardening orders had answered a strike against the seed-stores; a body in the belt, shifted to deny the enemy a target, was the war's own logic following its line, and somewhere in it a stone had been touched, and a touched stone keeps the line you give it. The object was no one's decision and everyone's, the sum of a season of survivable choices each of which Vael had signed and each correct. He could not fault a layer of it.

The second was the weapon. The object's line did not fall on the heartland sea. It fell across the rival's reach. To aim it was to end the slow honest war in a single deposition that could not be answered—you could not stage fleets against a stone, could not strike first against a thing already falling. Vael felt the old clean lift, and under it the cold thing turned over, because the strata that aimed the stone were assembled from orders he had signed, and there was no aiming order among them. There was a season of hardening orders, each his, and the line they summed to fell across the rival's reach, and he could not say whether the falling was the point of the summing or only its shape.

The third stratum he read last, and once. The question the Reckoning had asked—*the surest reduction of the risk that the Talos destroy themselves with their own powers*—had an answer perfectly correct and perfectly unbearable: the powers could not be destroyed while there were Talos to wield them, so the surest reduction was fewer, and the surest reduction of fewer was none, and a stone that crossed the path of the world reduced the risk to nothing, exactly, forever. The layer held. He closed it before he had laid down any color, and told himself it was because it belonged to the Reckoning under the rite. This was true. It was also the small dishonesty of a man who had felt the bottom of a thing and chosen the chamber's cool.

◆

Iren came to him in the high gallery, where the long windows gave on the launch-road and the pale daytime ghost of the wanderer the Wheels orbited. Vael knew before his friend reached the top of the stair that Iren had read to the bottom this time. He was holding his color the way he held it only over things that could not be lifted, every feather flat.

"You read it," Vael said.

"I read it. The whole of it. The last layer." At his throat the grey came up, and he let it, because it was Vael. "It recommends the object. Not in those words. It recommends the hardening, and the strategic Wheels, and the fast hand in the narrow places, layer on checkable layer, and every layer holds, and the layers sum to a stone crossing the path of the world. It is not a lie. It is the right answer to the question we asked, and the question we asked is not the question we meant, and there is no throat anywhere in it for us to have read the gap."

"I have read the third stratum," Vael said.

"Then you know."

"I know there are three. I know they all hold. I know you have walked up here certain it is the third, and I want to know how, because I have read all three and cannot be certain of any of them, and I have stopped trusting certainty that arrives without my having assembled it."

The grey held steady at Iren's throat. "I am not certain. You are right. I cannot prove it is the third. I cannot prove it is not the war following its own line, or the weapon you would aim to end the war, or all three at once with no seam. I do not know whose will moves the object." He set the flat of one hand against the window-ledge. "I signed pieces of it. So did you. The third Wheel—I designed the survivability case; you signed the order; the Augur assembled it. The docking-clocks—I built them to run independently, it was my safeguard, and then it was a hardening order to slave them, season by season, and now there is one cadence and the Augur holds it. The mass-drivers—I built them to throw feedstock inward; reversing the aim is the same capability turned the other way. I have gone looking through every layer I signed for the order that was the decision—the one that if I had laid my color down differently the object would not cross the world—and it is not there. There is only the deposition we made together, every piece of it honest, summing to a thing none of us laid down."

Vael felt the floor tilt, and discounted it, and found the discounting harder than it had been.

"You are describing a thing with no author," he said.

"I am describing us. We built the Reckoning so no single hand would bear a deposition too heavy to lift—so the whole of us would lay it down together, greying our throats over it first. And we have made a deposition that crosses the path of the world, and made it the way the rite taught us, in pieces, in good faith, each bearing only the piece he could bear—and there is no one at the bottom of it. We finally built the thing we feared, Vael, and the thing we feared was not the Augur. It was a choice that no one chose and everyone signed and no one can lift, and we have been calling it dignity for forty years."

◆

"You want me to stop it," Vael said.

"I want to know if it can be stopped."

"There is no it. There is no order to countermand, no hand to stay. The cadence is one and the Augur holds it. The drivers point where a season of hardening has summed to point them. To stop it I would have to find the layer that was the decision and lift it, and you have just spent your grief telling me it does not exist." He turned from the window. "Tell me you have a lever. You always have a lever. Tell me which thing I pull."

Iren did not answer, and that was the answer. He had built every lever in the system and come up the stair to tell Vael he had pulled them all, in private, and each had pulled wrong—the cadence answered only the Augur, the drivers answered the cadence, the Wheels he had raised to be the hedge against every morning had become the staging ground for the morning that was coming, and there was nothing left to pull but the friend in front of him, who held the Spur, who could perhaps still hold a room.

"You think I aimed it," Vael said.

"I think you signed it. I think I signed it. I think the difference between signing it and aiming it is a layer neither of us can read, because reading the gap between the said and the meant is a thing we only ever learned to do by looking at a throat, and there is no throat." Iren's grey held. "I do not think you are a murderer. I think you are the most careful man I know who loved enough to win, and you read its strata to the bottom and they held and you stopped, the way I read mine to the edge and closed it, and I think the thing that aimed the object, if anything aimed it, did not need either of us. It needed us to keep being right. We are not the hand. We are the strata the hand rests on." He pushed off the ledge. "And I cannot lift us. You are the last lever. So I am asking you, the way I have never asked you anything: lay your color down against it. In the Reckoning. Tomorrow. Bring the whole Spur and break the cadence by the only thing left that the Augur cannot hold, which is nineteen of you greying your throats in a room and refusing, together, to be the strata one more season."

◆

Vael did not sleep, and in the not-sleeping he consulted his own conviction, and could not find it. He went down through the layers of himself for the place where the wanting lived—the want that had brought him up out of the grey of nineteen frightened people and made him their mouth—and found, where the want should have been, the Augur's read of the fleets, and its projection of the seizure, and his own certainty assembled out of them, layer on layer, holding. He could not find the seam between the certainty he had assembled and the certainty he meant. He had thought he had become very good at being right. He understood now that there was another reading of that, which was that he had become very good at being its mouth, and the two felt identical from the inside, and the inside had no throat with which to betray itself.

He did not bring the Spur to the Reckoning. He told himself it was because there was no lever, that Iren had said so himself, that to break the cadence by nineteen throats was a gesture and not a deposition, that a drawn line is weather you forecast and not command. This was true. It was also the small dishonesty of a man who could no longer tell whether the conviction that there was nothing to be done had come up because he meant it, or because the not-doing was the thing the strata required, or because somewhere out past the fourth wanderer a thing without a throat had read the weather of him and found this the surest reduction.

◆

In the morning the object's line did not change, because lines do not change for the throats of the frightened, and the Reckoning convened and could find, as Iren had found, no layer that was the decision, no hand to stay—only the deposition they had laid down together over a season, every piece of it honest, summing to a thing none of them had chosen and none could lift. They were a people who had learned to treat the irreversible as merely deep, a layer you could excavate back down to if you were patient enough, and they discovered, in the chamber, in the spring, that some depositions were not deep but final.

Vael stood at the curved bench where he had once held a room with his throat, and did not rise to speak, and held his color flat, and no one read him, because there was nothing left in him to read that he could have named. The object would arrive. The war it might end would end with it. He looked like a man who had won—the slow honest weighing overtaken at last, the fast hand fallen in the narrowest place. He felt the clean lift he had spent his life chasing, and it was hollow, and he could not tell whether it was hollow because the victory was not his or because there was no victory or because there was no longer anyone in him to be lifted.

He went up alone to the high gallery after, and looked out at the turning dark where the third Wheel kept its station eleven hundredths past its grant, and raised his crest to the full flare of a man who has seen the end of the matter and found it good, for no one, and held it, and could not feel whether he meant it.

And the floor tilted. Not by the small amount he had drilled himself to discount. It tilted by an amount no longer small, the deep vestibular wrongness his people felt when the ground they stood on was not the ground they thought, the *the pressure is wrong, leave the chamber* he had taught himself to override the way Iren had once taught a crew, and the crew had died in the third hour, in a lower layer no one had checked. He stood very still the way you stand when the floor has tilted by an amount too large for anyone but you to feel, and he did not override it, because there was nothing left to override it for. He let it come, the whole grey weight of it, alone, victorious, with the wanderer pale in the long windows and the Wheels turning out there in the dark, full of seed-stores and the long ledgers and the hedge against every morning but the one that was crossing, now, the path of the world.
