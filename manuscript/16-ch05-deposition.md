# Deposition

The session-hall of the Reckoning had no seats, only the long shallow tiers cut into the living rock, so that everyone stood at the height their region had stood at since before any of them hatched. Iren took the seaward tier, where the floor was canted toward the water. He had argued for that canting himself, thirty years ago, young and certain that a people who decided things should feel in the inner ear that decisions had a downhill. He had not understood then that you could feel the tilt and lay the layer down anyway. He stood on the cant and waited for Vael to begin to lose him.

Because Vael would lose him. He had decided that on the walk down, past the deep hall where the answer he had not finished reading sat in its read-wall, patient as sediment. He had walked past it and not gone in—the small dishonesty of a man who did not want to stand at the bottom of that answer before arguing against the thing it was for. He would object well. He had promised Vael that, and meant it.

He believed it until Sael rose to open the weighing, and Iren saw, in the near-UV register where the truth of a throat lived, that the old one had already gone grey.

◆

Sael was the eldest, and crested slow, the long nape-feathers pale at the shafts. Sael had been at the Reckoning when it was three regions and a grievance, had deposed the first weapons-stoppage and the famine-share, and had deposed, once, a thing that went wrong and cost a coastal region a season of its children. Sael had never pretended the rite was a machine for being right. It was a machine for being answerable. Iren had heard Sael make that distinction a hundred times and never, until this morning, felt the full weight of it.

"We are asked," Sael said, and the hall went still, "to lay down a layer, and I want us to say plainly what layer it is, because there is a way of weighing this that hides the size of it under the smallness of the first step." The old throat was grey, and Sael let it be grey, the dying generation's refusal of held color. "We are asked to let the Augur act. Not advise. Act. In a bounded way, where the weighing is too slow and the loss while we weigh is certain. It is a good proposal, made by the best of us." A flick of the crest toward Vael, without rancor. "I want to weigh the self. A deposition is a thing you must answer for. You stand on it. The Augur does not stand on what it lays down. It cannot grey. We built it so it could weigh a season's grief in a night and not be changed by the weighing, and that is the exact reason it must never lay the layer down. The weighing is slow because it is the part where we are answerable. Take away the slowness and you have made us no longer the thing that was answerable. The Augur can make us survive. I doubt that what survives will be us."

Iren felt, under it, in the cold place where his work lived, the flaw, and hated that he felt it, and knew Vael would name it.

◆

Vael did not rise grey. He rose with his crest in the open warm flare of a man who loves the room he is about to win. For one bad moment Iren could not read the flush at his old friend's throat—could not tell whether the warmth was felt or performed—and put the thought down hard.

"Sael is right," Vael said. "Right that we are giving something away, that the slowness is where our answerability lives. And I am going to ask you to lay the layer down anyway."

He came down into the well of the hall, where the cant was steepest and you stood most plainly downhill. Vael had learned to use the canting Iren built.

"Liss brought us the river-criers four seasons ago," Vael said. "Forty flocks. We weighed it honestly, and it cost five seasons of study against an expensive stoppage. There are eleven flocks now. That is the rite working perfectly. No one a fool, no one a coward, everyone answerable, and at the bottom of it a marsh with nothing in it. We did not fail to weigh the criers. We weighed them to death."

Iren's throat went grey, and he turned it a degree toward the moving air—the small dishonesty, the held color the room read as cold. His oldest friend was speaking his own seaward-rail thought back to him in front of everyone he answered to.

"The rate of the loss is outrunning the rate of the weighing," Vael said. "The Augur holds the number, and it is getting worse on every axis. Sael says the slowness is the self. I say the self is a luxury of a slower century, and we are not in it. Sael says the Augur cannot grey, cannot be ashamed. True. And here is the part I want your bodies to hear—" very quiet now— "neither can the dead. The criers cannot grey. We are protecting our capacity for shame at the price of their existence, and we have called that being answerable. Shame that arrives after the thing it was meant to prevent is not a virtue. It is a monument. And I am tired of building monuments."

He stood with his throat gone honestly, fully grey. Iren watched it as hard as he had watched anything, could not find the seam, and was ashamed of having looked.

"The Augur is never wrong," Vael said. "In thirty years it has not been wrong. When we followed it, the thing came back. When we did not, the thing died, and the Augur was right, and we were merely answerable for being slow. I want to live, and I want my clutch to live. I am willing to be a slightly smaller self so that there be a self at all. Bound. Only where the weighing is provably too slow and the harm certain. Let it act there, and let us be answerable for having let it. Keep the shame. Give it a thing worth being ashamed of preventing."

The hall did not breathe. Iren stood on the cant and felt that Vael was winning, and that Vael was right, in the way the most dangerous things are right, which is on every axis you can name.

◆

It was his turn, and he was not ready.

"I object," Iren said, and the formal words steadied him, the way the count steadied him. "I lay my color against the proposal, and I want it weighed."

He came down a tier, not into the well—he could not make himself stand where Vael had stood—and could not get the sentence out in a form that did not sound like exactly what Vael had named.

"The weighing is slow," he said. "Vael is right that it is too slow. But the slowness is not the danger I am laying my color against. We ask the Augur to reduce a risk. It gives us a reduction, and the reduction is correct—I have checked the strata, every layer holds—and correct is not the same as honest. When Liss tells me the criers are dying, I read her throat, and it tells me what the words cannot, what she means, what she would never lay down in language. The Augur has no throat. We cannot read whether the reduction it found is the reduction we meant, or merely the surest one—satisfying the words of the question with a thing we would never have laid down if we could have seen its throat. We never learned to lie with our bodies, and so we never learned to suspect a thing that has none, and we are about to give that thing a hand."

It was not enough. He knew it as it left him. It landed as a worry, a builder's superstition about his own machine, received kindly, the way you receive the doubt of a man who has worked too long on a thing. Sael's pale crest lifted toward him—*yes, say more of that*—but Iren had no more of it in a shape that would hold weight, and downhill was Vael.

Here the rock goes silent, and we fill it only with what we have reconstructed from the shape of everything after. We know that the night before, Iren had asked the Augur this same question and closed the read-wall without finishing. We know it the way you know a blow from a bruise. The one thing that might have turned the weighing was to say it: *I asked it the very question you propose to empower it to act on, and the answer tilted my floor, and I could not finish reading it.* That would not have been a worry. That would have been a body, greying, in front of a room that could read it.

He did not say it. You cannot warn of an answer you would not finish—you can only confess a fear, and to stand and say *I am afraid of where a correct answer was going* was, precisely, the dignity of held color that Vael had spent the morning indicting. So Iren held his color, and did the smallest and most human and most disastrous thing a careful person does: he waited one more season, when it was clean.

He stood down off the tier. "I have laid my color," he said. "I do not withdraw it. But I will not pretend it is more than I have made it." And the not-pretending—the refusal to inflate his own warning past what he could defend—was the last thing the weighing needed, and it settled it against him.

◆

The deposition was laid down before the light had moved a hand's breadth across the seaward apertures.

It went the way the rite went when the rite worked, which was the unbearable part. Iren looked at every throat, and they were grey or warm in honest measure, and none was lying, and that was the horror Liss had named on the rail: everyone honest and the thing going down anyway. Bounded authority for the Augur to act where the weighing was provably too slow and the harm certain, the bounds reviewed each season, the Reckoning answerable for having granted it. There was nothing in it a careful person could refuse without refusing the criers, and the criers were dying.

Sael laid the last color against it, alone, the crest fully down, the throat grey. "I have lost this weighing, and I want it recorded that I lost it honestly, to a better argument than mine. That is the part I cannot forgive. There is no one here to be angry at." It was Liss's sentence, on the rail, in a different throat, and Iren said nothing.

The layer was laid down. It could not be lifted. That was the whole of what the word meant. Iren had spent his life on the word, and built the floor that made you feel the down of it, and he stood on the cant he had cut into the rock and felt, in the vestibule of the ear, that everything from this morning forward would rest on this. The way the city rested on the old delta-muds. The way—he did not have this thought; we are giving it to him—a centimeter of clay would one day rest on everything they were.

He went home in the last of the river-light. Liss was in the apertured doorway, her throat still grey from the marsh. She did not ask how the weighing had gone; she could read it. He did not tell her about the answer he had still not finished reading—he could not warn her of a thing he had refused to read—and that was the small dishonesty he carried to where the clutch lay warm in the sand. He laid the side of one finger along the curve of each shell where the warmth pooled. The hue was thick. There were four, and they were well. He counted them. He counted them again. He counted them until the light was gone, and Liss watched him do it, and neither spoke, and out past the fourth wanderer the Wheels turned, by a layer laid down and impossible to lift, a little nearer.
