# The Slow Way

From the south hide she could see the whole long-marsh at low water, and she counted the criers the way she had been taught to count anything that was leaving: not the birds, which moved and would cheat you, but the empty perches.

There were sixty-one channel-stakes in the near reach. In a good year a crier stood on every one at the turn of the tide, its long throat folded against the wind off the inland sea. This morning there were nine birds on the sixty-one stakes. She made herself look at each of the fifty-two bare ones, the way you look at a thing so that later you can swear you saw it. The empty stake was the true count. The bird was only the part that had not happened yet.

She set it down in the journal. Not the number alone, but the shape: the reach, the tide-state, the wind, the date in their own reckoning, and the thing she wrote more than any other, *not the dry*. The marsh was full to its margins and green to the waterline, and the criers were dying in it anyway.

She had stopped, two seasons ago, writing what she thought it meant. Meaning was for the Reckoning, and the Reckoning weighed it slowly, and the criers did not have the time. So she wrote what was, in a hand that did not editorialize, the way you write a name and a season on a marker and do not add that the one underneath was loved. The journal was kept for no one, against the day when there would be no one to keep it for. She kept it anyway.

A bird took one of the bare stakes while she watched, and for a moment there were ten, and her throat went its honest grey at the small mercy. She wrote *ten*, and then *one new arrival*.

◆

The thing in the water she could not see. That was the cruelty of it, what made it so easy for the honest to wait.

A drought you could see. It wrote itself on the mud in cracks a hatchling could read, and the Concord would act on a thing it could see. But the thing they had made and let go moved through the water, invisible. It did not kill the criers. It killed the small swimming lives in the mud the criers ate, down at the bottom where nothing voted, and the criers starved at the top of it slowly enough that you could tell yourself, every season, that this was a bad season and the next would be better, and be wrong.

She had opened a crier on the cold-stone two days ago, found stiff on the tide-line with no mark on it, its crop empty and its keel a blade under her fingers. Where the food should have been there was only the over-sweet smell of the water itself, the smell that meant the bottom of the marsh was wrong all the way down. She did not let her color go in the cold-room. She could not spend the grey on the dead when she would need it for the living, and that economy frightened her, because it meant she had begun to ration grief.

◆

She had brought it to the Reckoning four seasons ago, and they had weighed it honestly, and that was the part she could not get past.

She had not been refused by fools. She had stood in the deep hall with the criers' decline drawn on the read-wall in her own field-marks, and she had been heard. Sael himself had gone grey at her marsh and had said, in the formal mode, that the loss was real and the cause likely as she said. Then the weighing began—the stoppage of the works that fed the thing into the water set against the cost of the stoppage—and every region laid its color down, and the honest sum was: *study it. Be sure. The marsh is one marsh.*

And they were being sure. The study confirmed her to three places and would, in five seasons, confirm her to four, and somewhere in the fourth place the criers would be a thing it described in the past tense. She did not blame the regions. Each had weighed a true cost against a true cost. There was no villain in it and no error in it and the criers were going to die anyway. The weighing was honest. The weighing was too slow. The rite had been built by a people who had never before been able to kill the world faster than they could agree not to.

◆

Iren came to the hide near midday, which he never did, and she knew what it meant, because he did not leave the work-hall in daylight unless he had read something he could not sit with alone.

He stood in the low doorway with the marsh-light behind him and held his color the way he always did, that famous discipline the others read as coldness and she had learned to read as grief packed down so hard it had become load-bearing.

"You opened a bird," he said. "What was in it."

"Nothing was in it," she said. "That is what was in it." She had stopped softening things for him. They had never lied; it was the one wealth they had. "The bottom of the marsh is empty all the way down. The criers are starving in a full marsh. I can show you the line."

"I can read the line," he said, and her crest came up despite her, because *I can read a system* was the oldest fence between them. The line did not reach him, and the bird on the cold-stone was a thing he had not gone to look at. She thought that if he looked at it the way she had, he could not keep building the thing he was building, and that he had decided the building mattered more, and that he was probably right, and that she could not forgive him for being probably right.

"You did not come to read my line," she said.

He was quiet. His color did not move, and that was the answer, that what he had read in the hall was past the place where even she was allowed.

"The clutch is well," he said instead, which was not an answer and was the only true thing he had. "I counted them this morning."

"I know you did." She came to the doorway and stood with him, not touching, and she let her color go grey and he held his, and the gap between those two honesties was their whole marriage. "Iren. If the marsh goes, it goes slowly, and we will be able to tell ourselves the whole way down that the next season will be better. That is the thing I cannot bear. Not that it ends. That it ends so slowly we agree to it. I do not want the clutch to hatch into a world we have agreed to."

His crest folded flat against his nape, the way it did when he had heard a thing all the way to the bottom, and he did not say *bring it to the Reckoning*, which was the only mercy he gave her that day.

"No," he said. Only that. And then, because he was honest even when honesty was the cruelest thing in the hide: "I do not know how to build a thing that makes us decide faster than the loss without building a thing that decides for us." He said it almost to the marsh, and she did not understand all of it—she would, later, too late—but she understood the grey he was not showing, held so hard it had gone to stone.

She put the back of one hand against his nape. He let her. They stood.

*(We do not know that she said this. We have the journal, and a discipline too consistent to be anything but a life's practice, and a marsh that did die the slow way before the fast way came. We have given her the words a person keeps such a record in order not to say, because we cannot prove she did not, and the alternative is to leave her alone in the hide with the count.)*

◆

He went back to the work-hall before the light turned, because the work did not wait, and she stayed, because the marsh did not.

Near the turn of the tide a fisher's child brought her a crier still living, found down in the reeds, unable to rise. A young one, this season's, fledged late and never fed enough. She took it from the child's hands and felt the keel under the down, the blade of it, and carried it up into the last of the river-light at the seaward rail where she could see the empty stakes.

It did not struggle. That was the worst of it, that it had not the reserve left. It lay in the cradle of her hands with its long throat unfolded across her wrist and its tetracone eyes open on the water that was full and green to the margin and held nothing it could eat. Its color, what little a starving bird could still spend, went toward the marsh in the small involuntary signal that meant *here, this, the place*, the homing flush, the one a crier gave when it came down at evening onto its own reach. It only knew it was home.

She held it while it stopped, and she did not let her color go, and she hated that she could do this now, hold a dying thing with a dry throat, and she did it.

Then she laid the bird on the rail and opened the journal and wrote it: the reach, the tide, the wet warm air, *not the dry*. One young crier, this season's, dead of starvation in full water at the turn of the tide. The homing flush, given to an empty marsh. She wrote it in the hand that did not editorialize, and she did not write that she had loved it, because the record did not say such things and there was no one to say it to. She closed the journal and counted the bare stakes—fifty-two, the bird on the rail made it fifty-three—against the day there would be no one, and she kept the count anyway, because it was the only fidelity she had, and she would not let it happen in the dark.
