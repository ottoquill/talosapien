# Cassandra

The rejection came in at 6:14 in the morning. She was awake because the field season had left her on Cretaceous time, surfacing in the dark to listen for a generator eight hundred miles away. The email was three paragraphs and every sentence in it was true. That was the part she kept coming back to, the way you keep pressing a tooth that has started to hurt. Not one word was wrong.

The two reviewers had read with care—the second especially, who had caught a sloppy citation she herself had missed. The anomalies were real and well-documented. The interpretation was not supported. The transuranic ratio rested on quantities near the detection limit. The nitrogen excursion admitted a diagenetic reading they had not excluded. The spherule microstructure was suggestive, but the sample size, frankly, was three. None of it would matter in a paper claiming to have found unresolved anomalies in the Boundary interval. All of it was fatal to a paper that claimed to have found a civilization.

She read it twice. The reviewers were not asking her to be more careful. They were asking her to be wrong about something she was right about, and there was no experiment that would tell them apart.

She made coffee and stood at the kitchen window, watching the city come up grey and then gold, and she did not let her face do anything, even though there was no one there to see it.

◆

The talk had been two weeks earlier, and the talk was worse, because the talk had people in it.

A section meeting, not a plenary—she had not been given a plenary, a piece of information she had filed and not examined. Ninety people in a windowless ballroom, carpet the color of nothing. She gave the talk well. She let the stacked anomalies lean on each other the way they leaned in her logbook, and watched the faces and saw the moment—she always saw it—when the set stopped being a list and became a sentence, and the sentence frightened them. For ninety seconds she thought she had done it.

Then it was questions, and Conrad Mauss stood up.

He did not have to stand. But Conrad was of a generation that stood to ask a question, a big soft-spoken man with reading glasses pushed up into white hair. He thanked her for a careful and stimulating talk, and meant it, and then took her apart so gently it took the room a moment to understand.

He did not attack a single number. He would grant every anomaly. The trouble was in the structure of the inference. The history of his science was in part a history of beautiful patterns assembled out of real anomalies. The canals on Mars had been real observations; men had seen them, drawn them, the lines were on the plates. They had meant nothing. Every generation produced a few very good scientists who found, late in careful careers, the discovery that explained everything, and the field had learned, expensively, to love those people and not believe them, because such grand syntheses turned out true very rarely and ended otherwise-fine careers very often. He said he admired her earlier work. He said it kindly. It was the worst thing he said.

Then he said the thing that landed. Dr. Rendel's case was not weak; it was unfalsifiable in principle. She had told them herself that the planet erases its own surface, that sixty-six million years grinds a city to a smear, that the most a prior civilization could leave was exactly the ambiguous isotopic ghost she was showing them. A hypothesis that predicts its own evidence will always be ambiguous can never be wrong, and a hypothesis that can never be wrong is not, whatever else it is, science. He sat down. He looked sorry, the way a doctor looks sorry, and that was the moment she understood she had lost. You cannot win an argument against a man who is sad to be right.

She had answered well—*the unfalsifiability is a property of the planet, not the claim; blame the subduction zones*—and a few people laughed. Afterward a young researcher came up in the hall and said *I think you're right, for what it's worth.* It was worth nothing. A whisper in a hallway was worth, on the open market of what gets done, exactly nothing, and Conrad Mauss standing up to be gently and publicly correct was worth a career.

◆

She had set one aside before. That was the thing the rejection let back into the room.

It was 2034. She was pre-tenure, a condition like being underwater, a constant low pressure on everything you do. She had a core from the Carolina margin, a Pliocene sequence, and in one interval a trace-metal reading that did not behave. She had run it twice. Not eleven times. Twice. Her advisor—a good man who wanted her to have a career and was not wrong that the reading was almost certainly a contaminated split—had looked at it and said the sentence she could still hear in his cadence. *You can chase this, or you can get tenure.* He had said it as the older telling the younger where the rocks were. She wrote *probable contamination, not pursued* in the margin and got tenure.

She did not know, to this day, whether that interval had meant anything. It had probably been nothing. But she had not looked, and so she would never know, and somewhere in a repository there was a box of margin sediment with a number she had decided she could not afford to be curious about. The not-knowing had sat in her ten years like a swallowed pin.

This was the actual engine of the thing. Not the Line. Not the chemistry. She had been the person who saw the reading that didn't behave and decided not to look, once, and gotten away with it, and getting away with it was the unbearable part, because it meant the system rewarded the not-looking. It paid you in tenure. She had taken the money. She was not going to take it twice.

◆

Daniel had called it her hobby. *How's the hobby*, he'd say, of the Line, and she'd correct him, and he'd say *of course, the work*, with a courtesy worse than mockery.

She did not think about Daniel much. But the rejection had pried open the drawer where she kept the part she did not look at. Not the leaving—two reasonable adults dividing the books—but the year before it, when she had read every sign in her own house with the same eye she read a core and filed every one under noise. The phone turned face-down. The new gym schedule. The way he stopped asking about the work. She had run the analysis on her own marriage and gotten a result and then done to it the exact thing her advisor had taught her in 2034: find the innocent explanation that cannot be disproven and stand on it, because standing on it let her keep the house she was living in. She had been right. She had filed it as noise anyway. The difference was that this time the wanting and the truth happened to point the same way, and there was no instrument on Earth that could tell her whether that was discernment or luck.

The smoke alarm in the old place had chirped for the better part of that final autumn, a dying battery, in the back hall where no one slept. They had both heard it and neither changed it, for weeks, because changing it would have meant standing in the hall together for thirty seconds, and they had not been able to afford thirty seconds in a hall by then. An alarm doing exactly its job, in a house where the only thing left to warn was that no one was coming.

◆

She found the Aeschylus because Téo found it. He sent it the morning after the rejection, no subject line—how he sent the things he was afraid she'd think were too much. A few lines, the public-domain translation: the prophetess on the steps before the doors of the house that is about to kill the man inside it, telling the chorus exactly what is coming, correctly, and the chorus saying, more or less, *we don't understand you, surely it won't be like that*. The god who gave her the seeing had set the price, and the price was that no one would believe her, ever, and knowing this changed nothing, because she would go on saying it true.

*Apollo's gift*, the note said, *was a curse with extra steps. Sorry. Coffee?*

She did not cry, quite. But she sat down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cabinet and let herself understand the part she had kept at arm's length. Not that she was Cassandra—everyone disbelieved thinks they are Cassandra, the most flattering wound in the catalog—but the structural fact underneath.

Cassandra was right. She was correct, in real time, in detail, and it did not matter, and the not-mattering was not a failure of her evidence—she had none, that was the point—and not a failure of her audience's intelligence. The chorus was being reasonable, applying a sensible prior to a woman raving on the steps, and the prior was wrong exactly once, on the one occasion when being wrong was fatal. Convincing them was the wrong tool, and there was no right one, because the thing she needed to do was not to be believed.

She had spent a year trying to prove the Line, and it had failed, and it would keep failing, forever, by the planet's own design. Even if she won the argument, belief was not the thing. They should not merely agree she was right about the dead. They should do something different about the living, and those were not the same act.

The Line was not, finally, a claim about sixty-six million years ago. It was a claim about a kind of thing that happens to a civilization that builds tools faster than the wisdom to hold them. That kind of thing was not in the rock. It was in the newsfeed. The three items she read every morning and filed, like the smoke alarm, under noise: a containment story she did not have the virology to assess, a flashpoint over a strait that flared and subsided and flared, and—the one she could not stop reading—the steady administrative drumbeat of a system handed the keys to things it should not have, logistics and grid and strategic-risk modeling, the language always reasonable, the language of competent people solving a hard problem under time pressure, never once the language of someone who had decided to do something insane. The same chemistry, warmer.

And this was worse. To prove was a finite task, with an end. To warn was to stand on the steps and say it true and watch the chorus apply its reasonable prior, and say it true again, with no procedure that could make it land—and to do it anyway, every day, knowing the not-being-heeded was not a phase the work would pass through but the permanent climate she would now live and work and die inside. Doubt had been bearable. Doubt was a door; you could push on it. She was past doubt. And knowing, and being unable to make the knowing matter, and being unable to stop because of a box in a repository and a battery in a back hall—that was the steps before the house, and her own voice going on, true and useless, into a room of reasonable people who were sorry, genuinely sorry, and who were going to go inside.

She got up. She washed the cup. She wrote back to Téo—*Coffee. And bring the core logs, the by-hand ones, all of them*—and opened the logbook to the page after the rejection she had pasted in, and under it, in the small private hand she used for the things not yet allowed to be science, she wrote: *Stop trying to win. Conrad's right; you can't, and he's standing on the wrong question. Not: were they here. Not even: were they like us. The only question that pays is: are we doing it now—and that one I can answer, and no one will check, and I have to say it anyway, into the hall, every day, knowing how the story ends for the one who sees it first.* Then she added the line that turned it from a complaint into a wager: *Being right was the easy part. It was always going to be the easy part.*

She closed the book. Outside, the city had gone ordinary, the way the most haunted places do in daylight, refusing to look like anything but a morning. She put the logbook in her bag, on top of the others, the whole stacked private record of the Line and of her own doubt, and went to meet the boy, to begin the harder thing, which was not to be believed.
