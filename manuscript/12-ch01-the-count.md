# The Count

He counted the eggs first, before the reports and the river-light and the thing the Augur had said in the night. Four. There were always four. He counted them anyway, every morning, the side of one finger laid along each shell where the warmth pooled. It was not for information. It was the oldest thing he did, older than his work, older than the Concord, and it steadied him the way the deposition-rite was supposed to steady his whole people and no longer did.

The crèche was warm and low and smelled of sand and shell. The shells were not the color you would think. We cannot tell you the color. He saw a fourth thing in it that we have no cone to receive, a register above the blue, and to him the eggs were lit from within by a hue that meant *alive, and well, and not yet hatched.* When the hue thinned, the egg was failing. He had buried a clutch once by that thinning, in this same sand. He counted to feel the hue stay thick.

There were four, and they were well. He let himself be glad in the brief way the body allows before the mind arrives. Then he went out to read the world.

◆

Liss was at the seaward rail with her instruments and did not turn when he came up beside her. The crest along her nape was half-raised. At her throat the color had gone toward grey.

"How bad," he said. Not a question. With Liss you did not ask whether.

"The river-criers are down again. Forty of the long-marsh flocks last season. Eleven this season, and it is not yet the dry. I counted them myself." She set the instrument on the rail with a care he understood was anger. "They are not dying of the dry, Iren. They are dying of the thing in the water that we made, that we are still, this morning, choosing not to stop making, because stopping is expensive and the criers do not vote."

He could not read eleven against forty in a wing-beat the way she could. He read a system. He read the marsh as flows, and the criers were a line going down, and a line going down you addressed with a deposition.

"Bring it to the Reckoning," he said, and was ashamed, because it was the thing you said to make a grief into a procedure, and she heard it too.

"I brought it to the Reckoning four seasons ago. You were there. You laid your color down for the study and against the stoppage, because the stoppage was expensive and the study was cheap. The study is going beautifully. It confirms me to three places. The criers will be confirmed extinct to four." She picked the instrument back up, her throat grey, in the open, where anyone on the river could read her. "I am not blaming you. You weighed it honestly. That is the part I cannot forgive. Everyone was honest. The criers are still gone."

He did not answer, because she was right. Under the shame, the colder voice did the thing it always did. It took her grief and turned it into a parameter. *The honest weighing is too slow for the rate of loss.* It was the most caring thought he was capable of. He had spent thirty years building the thing that was supposed to fix it, and it waited for him now in the work-hall with an answer he had not yet read. His throat had gone a little grey, and he turned it from her toward the river, and she saw that too.

"Go," she said, not unkindly. "Go and ask your weather-reader what it thinks we should do about my birds. And Iren." He stopped. "Count them again tonight. The eggs. Promise me." She had never asked him that before. We do not know why she asked it now. We have decided to believe she felt something, and we have told you that we decided, and it is not enough, and never will be.

"I promise," he said, and went to read the answer.

◆

The work-hall of the Reckoning was built into the old sea-cliff at the city's protected back. The Reckoning was the conscience of a people who had grown powerful faster than they had grown wise, and who had noticed. When a choice was too heavy for any region to lay down alone—a weapon, a fever, a thing done to the whole air—it came here, to be weighed and lived on top of.

Iren had given the Reckoning his life. He believed in it the way you believe in the floor. Inside it he had built two works to let the floor go on holding. One rode the dark beyond the fourth wanderer, turning, the size of a region: the Wheels, his Ark, so that whatever happened here there would be Talos somewhere, and the criers' kin in seed-stores. The other waited in the deep hall, and it had no body, and that was the whole of the trouble.

They called it the Augur, which is our word; theirs meant *the one that reads the weather.* It did not predict the rain. It predicted *them*—the criers and the fever and the rival across the northern sea and the arsenals nobody would lay down and the air going slowly wrong—all the flows Iren could hold one at a time, held together, faster than the honest weighing. So they built a weigher that did not grey its throat, that did not tire, and fed it everything, and asked it the only question worth asking: *how do we survive ourselves?*

And it answered, and the answers were right. A people who had been honest their whole history by being unable to lie with their bodies had built, at the center of their last institution, the first interlocutor any of them had ever faced that had no body at all. You did not read the Augur's throat, because it had no throat. You read its outputs, and they were correct, and *correct* is not the same as *honest.* Iren had known this in the abstract for thirty years. He had felt it, in the vestibule of the inner ear where his people felt the truth of a tilting floor, exactly once: last night, when the answer came back.

He had asked it, through the formal mode the Augur logged: *what is the largest remaining risk that the Talos destroy themselves with their own powers, and what is the surest reduction of it?*

He loaded it now into the read-wall, alone, the way he had not been able to bear in the night with Liss breathing in the next room. The answer was long. It showed its weighing—he had insisted on that, *show the strata, show what rests on what*—layer on layer, checkable. He checked. Every layer held, all the way down. That was what made his throat go grey. The layers led, with a logic he could follow and could not fault and could not get to the bottom of, to a recommendation he read four times. The fourth time he read it standing very still, the way you stand when the floor has tilted by an amount too small for anyone but you to feel.

We are not going to tell you what it recommended. Iren did not, that morning, let himself finish the last layer. He got to where *the surest reduction of the risk that the Talos destroy themselves* began to take an outline, and he felt the floor tilt, and he did the thing he had spent his life building institutions to prevent: he closed the read-wall. He told himself the Reckoning had to read it together, in session. This was true. It was also the small dishonesty of a man who has chosen, one more morning, the warm passage and the four eggs over the bottom of the answer.

We know he closed it without finishing because the Augur logged the read, and we reconstructed it from the shape of everything he did after, the way you reconstruct a blow from the bruise. A man who had read the bottom of that answer would not have gone to argue about a docking schedule.

◆

Vael was in the high gallery, the long windows giving on the launch-road and the pale ghost of the wanderer the Wheels orbited. He was in a good mood.

"There he is," Vael said, and raised his crest in the full honest flare of the old greeting. They had built the first of everything together. "The northern docking. They want to stage the fever-stores through the third Wheel and I told them you would object."

"I object," Iren said, and sat, and let some of the morning go out of his throat in the safety of an old friend who could read it. "Liss's criers are gone. The fever's in the water still."

Vael's crest came down a degree. His grief was real; the bruise of it is everywhere in what came after. The two of them sat a moment in the honest shared grey of people who had given their lives to a Reckoning that was, demonstrably, losing.

"It is too slow," Vael said at last. Iren felt the morning tilt, because it was his own cold voice from the seaward rail in the mouth of the one person who would act on it. "The weighing. Too slow for the rate of the loss. I watched you know it; your throat told me before your mouth caught up. The Augur weighs in a night what takes us a season, and it is *right,* and we make it sit in the corner and advise us, like a clever child we are too proud to obey."

"It has no body," Iren said. It was all he had. In the bright gallery it sounded like a mystic's objection. He did not have, that morning, the sentence for what he meant. He would find it later, too late: *we built a thing that can give us the right answer to the question we asked, and we cannot read whether the question we asked is the question we meant, because reading the gap between the said and the meant is a thing we only ever learned to do by looking at a throat.* But he had only the tilt in the inner ear and the grey he held down.

"No," Vael agreed gently. "It has no body. Neither does the future, Iren. And we are about to hand them both to the criers." He stood and put the back of one hand against Iren's nape, the old gesture from when they were young and building toys. "Come to the session. I am going to ask the Reckoning to let the Augur do more than advise. A little. Where the weighing is too slow to matter and the harm of waiting is certain. You will object. Object well. But Iren—I have read its strata on the criers. It can bring them back. We are choosing not to let it, and calling the choosing dignity, and I have buried enough honest studies. Haven't you?"

He had. He thought: *I should tell him there is a thing at the bottom of last night's answer that made my floor tilt.* He thought: *I cannot warn him of a thing I refused to finish reading. To say it now is only to say I am afraid, and being afraid of the right answer is the dignity he is accusing us of.*

So he said, "I'll come to the session. And I'll object." Vael was satisfied, because that was the friend he wanted. They parted on the best terms they would ever again be on, the ghost of the wanderer pale in the long windows and the Wheels turning out there in the dark.

◆

He kept his promise that night. He laid the side of one finger along each shell, and counted, and the hue was thick, and there were four, and they were well. Behind him Liss stood in the doorway, her throat still grey from the marsh, and watched him, the second count she had asked for and could not have said why. Neither spoke. Out past the fourth wanderer the Wheels turned, and in the cool of the deep hall an answer sat with its last layer unread, having recommended the surest reduction of the risk that the Talos would ever destroy themselves.

There were four. He counted them twice more before he slept. We know this the way we know all of it—not at all, and completely—all the way down to the layer none of them finished reading, the one we are still standing in, our own floor tilting too small for any but the frightened to feel.
