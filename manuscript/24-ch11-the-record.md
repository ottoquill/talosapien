# The Record

She went out before the river-light to count the spring-callers, and there was one.

She had known it for eleven days, since the morning the south-shoal pair did not answer the dawn. The spring-callers had answered the dawn in this marsh for every season of her life, and her line's life before her, back past memory into what is only deposited and never recalled. The marsh set itself by them. For three mornings she had waited to learn whether the one was the male or the female. If the female, there might be a clutch in the reeds she had not found. If the male, there would not, and the species had already ended, on a morning while it was still calling. A male calling alone into a marsh with no female is already the past. He simply has not been told.

It was the male. She knew his voice, the flaw in the third phrase, the dropped note that ran true down the male side. Years ago, when she still believed the marsh would outlast her, she had thought of it as the marsh signing the dawn in its own hand. He gave it now from the dead reed at the shoal's edge, the call perfect and answered by nothing, and she stood at the rail of the south hide with her instruments and did not raise them. There was nothing left to measure. She had measured everything. She had laid the numbers down where the Concord could read them and not act, and the numbers had been right and the spring-callers were gone anyway. Now there was only the one act the numbers could not perform. To be there.

She did the thing she had decided long ago she would do. She took out the record.

It was not an instrument. It was the keeping-book that was hers and not the Reckoning's. The reconstruction knows of it only as it knows the count of the eggs—from the shape of a habit pressed into everything around it, never from the book, which the rock did not keep. We have invented its existence because a woman who measured this much would have kept a place for what measuring could not hold. *We are guessing at this. We guess because the alternative is to leave her alone in it.* She made the entry plainly, without the grief greying her throat, because the grief was hers and the record was the marsh's. She set down the place, the morning, the flaw in the third phrase, so that whatever read this would know it had been a particular animal and not a category. Then she wrote the last thing the record was for: that there had been one, and that she had heard him, and that after him there would be none.

She closed the book. The male called again behind her as she walked back, and she did not turn. Turning would have been for her, and this morning was not for her.

The clutch turned in the night.

She felt it before she heard Iren feel it, the change in the warmth under the breast of the crèche, the shift of weight inside a shell that is no longer only a shell. She lay in the dark of the warm passage with her crest flat against the sand and let the dread come up the way the marsh-water came up through the reeds, from below, without a sound. The clutch would hatch within a hand of days. The warmth said *soon*, and soon meant that four small persons would open their mouths into the air she had measured, the air with the thing in it, on a marsh going silent one signature at a time.

There is a dread that belongs only to a parent who has read the data. It is not the fear that the young will die. It is the colder thing under it: the suspicion that you are not saving them by making them, only enlarging the count of what will be lost.

She got up before the warmth could pull more of her color and laid the side of one finger along each shell where the warmth pooled, the way he did. Four, the hue thick and rising, alive and very nearly here. She held her throat from going grey over them. The shells did not need her grey. The shells needed her warm.

◆

Iren found her there. He came down the passage and read her, the long patient systems-read, and she let him, and her throat had gone grey despite the will.

"How bad," he said.

"The spring-callers are gone. There is one. He was calling this morning. He will call until he dies and then the marsh will set its dawn by nothing." She put the finger back along the third shell, where the hue ran strongest. "And the clutch turned in the night. Within a hand of days."

She watched him lay the two facts side by side as a system, the loss and the coming-into-being, and watched the cold voice arrive behind his eyes, the one she had married. She did not hate him for it anymore, which was worse than hating him.

"I am going to bring the air-work to the session," he said. "The full set. Yours. The spring-callers are the same argument with one more body—" He stopped. He had been about to say *the body counts*, and they both heard where the sentence had been going, and he did not finish it.

"Don't. Not the session. I bring it every season. I brought the criers and they gave me a study. I will bring the spring-callers and they will give me a study, and the study will confirm me to four places, and there will be no spring-callers, and there will be a beautiful record of how honestly we watched them go." She set the keeping-book on the sand between them, where he could see it for what it was. "This is what I do now. I write it down, so that the marsh signed its last dawn in a hand and not a number. That is the only thing I can still give them. I cannot give them the marsh. I gave up the marsh four seasons ago. I just did not tell myself the date."

His throat, which he held famously, went a shade she had seen perhaps five times in their bond, and he did the thing he always did with the unbearable. He tried to build past it.

"Then it is not enough," he said, gently, and the gentleness cut, because he was not arguing. He was grieving in the only language he had. "A record is a thing you keep for after. I want a before where they are not gone. I cannot keep a true account of a death. I have it in me to build the thing that stops it, or a smaller thing, and a smaller thing, until the last thing I can build is a wall around four eggs. You keep the dying. I keep the living. We are doing the same work from opposite ends and it does not meet in the middle and I am so tired."

"There is a middle," she said. "It is the eggs. They are exactly the middle." And his crest came down all the way, flat.

"I know," he said. "I count them in the morning."

"I counted them tonight. Before you woke. I have started doing it. I did not used to."

He looked at her a long time in the thin light, and what passed between them the reconstruction cannot keep, and we will not pretend it can. Two people who had laid down more strata in common than any two minds in their world, each unable to do the other's only act of love, each refusing to call the other wrong.

"Neither of us is wrong," she said at last, which was the cruelest thing she could say, the one that left no one to blame. "You build because building is your love. I keep the record because the record is mine. And the marsh dies, and the clutch comes, and neither of us will save them, and we will both have been faithful. That is the part I cannot forgive. The honesty. We are both going to have been so honest."

◆

The male called for three more mornings, and on the fourth he did not. She went out to the south hide and listened to the marsh set its dawn by nothing, the strangest sound she had ever not heard, the dawn coming up unsigned over reeds that no longer answered the light. This was what the world would sound like soon, all the way out. The record of a thing dies with the last one who can read it, and she was the record, and she would not be kept either.

She took out the keeping-book and made the last entry, plainly, on the open rail where anyone on the river could read her, because she had decided long ago that holding your color was a luxury the dying could not afford from her. She set down the morning, and that the call had not come, for the first time since before there were words to set it down in. She wrote only the count, which had been one, and was now none, and closed the book.

Then she went home, down the warm passage, and laid the side of one finger along each shell where the warmth pooled, and the hue was thick and very near to breaking, almost here, and she did not hold her throat. She let it go grey over them, in the dark, with no one to read her, the grey of a woman who had read the data and made them anyway. She counted them, because the counting was the oldest thing she did, older than her work, older than the record, a motion her body had inherited from animals that could not have told you why they made it either. There were four. They were well. They were coming into a world already ending, and she stayed with her finger on the warm shell long after she knew the count. It was the only kind of keeping the marsh had left her, and the only kind she had ever been any good at: not the saving of a thing, which she had never managed, but the refusal to let it go unwitnessed into the dark.
