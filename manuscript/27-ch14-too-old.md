# Too Old

The piece broke off in his glove, and that was how he knew, before the chemistry, that it was not a rock.

He had gone back. The not-going-back had been the lie, and a man could only hold a lie that long with the file on the good screen every shift, dark and corrugated and patient. He had spent delta-v on worse credit than before, three passes and a rendezvous. Now the *Margin* hung a hundred meters off the bad-orbit object and Roan was out on the tether with a sample bag, the headlamp on a surface that drank the light.

He had meant to chip a sample, log the geometry, do the assay. Then, only then, you knew what you had. He set the chisel against a seam—a seam, on a rock—and tapped it, and a flake the size of his palm came away clean. Clean the way nothing in nature comes away. Clean the way a thing comes apart along the line where it was joined.

He held it in the lamp. Too light for metal, too dark for ice, and on the broken face a layering, thin bright laminae against a matrix that drank the beam, that no sediment laid down and no melt froze. He turned it. The light found a straight edge drawn straight on purpose. A ruled line. A manufacture.

Out here you did not say things out loud. The recorder caught everything and you kept your mouth shut until you knew. He put the flake in the bag and went back along the tether, the second couch empty for no one to be told.

◆

He ran the assay that night, and the assay was the part he understood, which was a small mercy.

The *Margin* had a mass spectrometer because a survey ship had to, a prospector's instrument built to tell nickel from iron from olivine for the rock-broker desk. It was not built for this and Roan made it do this anyway, the way he made the heater loop run on docking grease, by knowing its lies. He ran a fragment, then ran it again with a fresh cut, because the first numbers were not ones a prospector saw.

The metals first. The alloy—and it was an alloy, a made mixing, not an ore—was light and strong, the proportions of someone solving a problem Roan knew the shape of: the problem he solved every time he chose a member for a stressed joint, mass against strength against the cold. Aluminum and titanium. What an engineer reached for to build something that turns in the dark and holds air.

Then the isotopes, and the isotopes were where the floor went out.

He had a prospector's nose for a number that should not exist, and the spectrometer kept handing him, in the trace stuff under the alloy, ratios that no ore carried and no ice and no chunk of a shattered parent body. There were daughters with no natural parents—heavy lines that on Earth came out of a reactor or a bomb and nothing else—and their proportions were skewed, every one, in the same direction. The direction of a great deal of decay, of clocks running a very long time. He looked the half-lives up and did the arithmetic on the back of a survey log with a stub of pencil, because he did not trust the screen not to be lying.

The arithmetic said tens of millions of years.

He erased it and did it again, slower, with the other lines, the ones that decayed on a different schedule, because the beauty of more than one clock was that they had to agree or you'd made a mistake. They agreed. Two clocks on different chemistry, and what they agreed on was a number with seven zeros, a number that belonged to the Boundary clay and the dinosaurs, not to a thing made with a ruled line and an alloy chosen to hold air.

Made things were not old. That was the whole order of the universe as Roan had lived in it. Old things were rock. Made things were new, were ours, the thin leading edge of a frontier started about the time his grandfather was born. The two facts did not sit in the same universe, and he sat there holding both.

◆

The first thing he thought about was what it was worth, and he forgave himself for it the way you forgive a reflex.

A made thing was salvage, and salvage had rates. A made thing that was *not human*—because a thing this old could not be human—was salvage with no rates yet, which meant the rates were whatever the first man to file made them. He lived in that for a minute. The debt gone. The variance fees, the premium for flying Devi's couch empty, the note Mara worried about over fourteen minutes of lag—gone, on a single filing.

Then the cold thing started to arrive, a sensor being right about something it could not understand.

He had been thinking of it as a find. *Wreck.* The word turned wrong the way the flake had, because wreck meant a ship, and a ship meant a builder, and the builder had laid up an alloy against the cold and the spin fifty million years before there was a hand on Earth to hold a chisel. He thought of the renderings Mara loved, the green cylinders, the rivers curving up the walls of the world. *Somebody has to find them the rocks to make the cans out of,* she had said. *It could be us.*

He looked at the radar shadow in the corner of the screen, the long axis too long, the corrugation his processing had kept failing to call noise. He had looked at it for weeks as an outlier. He looked at it now as a piece of a wall that had once curved up over someone's head, the periodicity the regular structure of a thing built to turn. The not-human builder had built the exact thing the renderings promised, sixty million years gone.

*This is not ours,* he thought. *And it is not new.* Neither able to be true if the other was.

◆

He almost did not write it up, and the almost was Devi, and that was the worst of it.

She got in the cabin the way she got in the cabin, sideways, more certain than he was. *File it, Iyer. You don't sit on a thing that's made and old. That's not a claim.* He started to argue the money at her and it died in his mouth, because the money was the small thing. That was the shape the grief took. Not the big absence. The small one: the find of his life, and no one in the cockpit to say *Dev, look at this.*

The second couch was behind him. He did not turn to it. You learned not to.

He could file it as salvage and keep his mouth shut about the age. That kept the zeros, and no one ran isotopes on salvage, the way no one ran transuranics on a Boundary sample.

He filed the other thing instead, by the procedure. He wrote the assay up straight. The alloy. The structure, the ruled line, the seam that came apart clean. Every isotope line, the skew of the daughters all in the one direction. The cross-check: two clocks, different chemistry, in agreement. He wrote the number with its seven zeros and did not soften it and did not editorialize it, because the moment you editorialized an extraordinary return they got to stop reading you. He attached the radar shadow and wrote: *Object is manufactured. Object is old. I cannot reconcile these and am not going to try.*

He posted it to the open board, not the private channel. That was the part that cost him the zeros, because the moment the isotopes were public the rock stopped being his. He sat with his thumb over the key, and then pressed it, and the assay went into the lag toward the inner system at the speed of light, fourteen minutes to Mara and the broker desk and then onward, indexed, searchable, into the standing pool of numbers.

He did not know that an isotope ratio is a thing a particular kind of scientist runs a standing alert on. That fourteen light-minutes and then some down the gradient there were geochemists who kept a net out for exactly the heavy lines he'd measured. That one would see a Belt prospector's filing in a feed it had no business in, feel the cold of a number behaving the way it was not allowed to, and forward it to a paleo-geochemist told for a decade that what she read in a centimeter of clay could not be there.

The threads did not touch. They leaned. Two halves of a sentence written sixty-six million years apart drifted closer to lying in the same line, and no one yet held both ends.

◆

"Found something that isn't gravel," he told the recorder, that evening, for Mara, fourteen minutes away.

He had practiced not saying more. The lag did his arguing for him. He left it a moment.

"It's old, Mara. I ran the dates twice on two clocks and they agree and I don't like what they agree on. I put the assay up on the open board. The whole thing, the isotopes, not the private channel. I know what that costs. Don't—" He stopped. He had been going to say *don't tell Okonkwo*, the reflex, the claim, the zeros. He let it go. "Tell whoever. It's not ours to sit on. Somebody built it. It's not us, and it's not new, and those don't go together, and they're both true, and I checked."

He looked at the second couch, finally, the way you look at a thing once and put it away.

"Devi'd have made me file it," he said. "She was always right about the ones about other people."

He sent it. He sat in the webbing in the cold with the loop holding on the wrong grease, and outside the hull the made thing turned, slow, fifty million years past the warmth of whatever hand had laid the alloy up against the dark. What Roan felt was not the zeros and was not discovery. It was the cold coming in. Not the cabin's cold; that he could fix, by headlamp, the way he fixed everything. The other cold. The slow arrival of a man who has let a true number all the way in, and knows it is going to cost him more than everything he came out here to keep.
