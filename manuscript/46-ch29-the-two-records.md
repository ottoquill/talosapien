# The Two Records

The keeping-book was not in the crèche. She had stopped keeping it there when the count went to none for the criers, because she could not write a none in the room where the four were loud. So it was on the seaward rail in its wrap of treated hide, left open to the count of the day, where the surge found it. We will follow it down, because we can. It is the one thing in the whole account we can follow further than we followed him.

The wall of water took the rail and the warm stone and the wrap of hide and the book inside, and rolled all of it inland in the dark, in a slurry of river-mud and sand and the falling sky already cooling out of the air. The medium she wrote in—we do not know what it was; we have never found one; we are inferring the book itself from the habit of a woman we invented—came apart in the water the way everything she loved came apart, slowly and then all at once. When the surge slowed and let its burden fall, what settled over the drowned delta was a single graded bed of everything the city had been, mixed past separation. Her record was in it, indistinguishable from the mud, because it had become the mud.

There is no page down there. The keeping-book is lost the way a word is lost that was spoken into a wind sixty-six million years ago. We have the bed it dissolved into, and in that bed the fixed nitrogen and the split atom's daughters and the glass that should be formless—the chemistry of the world she was keeping the record *of*. We have the death she documented, written in the only ink that lasts, the ink she did not choose and could not read. But the keeping, the hand laid against the loss in long love, is gone, into the same centimeter that kept the poison. She had always said the record was the marsh's. She was righter than she meant.

◆

The logbook was in Asha's bag, the second one, with the margin name she would not now ever erase. She carried it into the building where the hearing had ended and she did not yet know how, and set it on the table in the small room they had given her to wait in, and did not open it. She had decided on the drive in that the next hand to open it would not be hers.

Téo was already there, by the window with the winter light behind him. He did not ask how it had gone. Her face was doing the thing she never let it do.

"I want you to have this," she said, and put her flat hand on the cover, the way you put a hand on a thing you are keeping. "Not the data. The data's published, or rejected, or both; it can take care of itself. This is the other record, the one I kept by hand. It's got the names in it." She heard her voice do something and held it. "I named them, Téo. The dead. I gave a drowned maker a name out of the metal in the clay, and the four beside him a number I have decided is not a number, and I wrote under it, *there were four, he was not alone when it came.* I know it isn't science. I've stopped being able to tell whether that's the worst thing in the book or the only true thing in it."

Téo did not reach for it, because she had taught him to assume the thing he most wanted was the thing most likely to be lying, and he was applying it to the wanting itself.

"Why me," he said. "Why not the archive. A vault, a server, ten servers. Why not put it somewhere it can't be lost."

"Because that's not what a record is for." She took her hand off the cover. It was harder than she had thought. "I used to think you kept it so someone could later prove the thing. Then I spent a year learning it can't be proven, that the whole shape of it is a real case that's permanently deniable." She pushed it the last inch. "It's for the next hand. A record no living hand opens is just a tombstone. The only thing that makes it a letter is you taking it."

He picked it up then, not like a deliverable, like a thing that was warm, and she let her face do, in front of him, the thing it had been holding through the whole of the room she had just lost in.

"You're not erasing the names," he said. It was not a question.

"No."

"Even though it isn't science."

"*Because* it isn't science," she said. "This is the part that's only true if somebody keeps it. I kept it. Now you keep it. There's just whether the next hand opens it in time."

◆

Here is the thing we have to say plainly, once.

The two records are the same record. We will not insult you by pointing at the place in the rock where the figure repeats. They are the same act performed twice by the same kind of creature on opposite sides of a centimeter of clay: laying a hand against a loss you cannot stop and keeping faith with it, because the keeping is the fidelity, and the fidelity does not require an after to be worth doing. Iren found the word for it on the shoal, and it was *fidelity*. Asha found the same word and called it the next hand. It is one act.

And one record was kept, and one was lost, and the difference is not in the records. Liss wrote in the only medium her world gave her and it dissolved into mud. Asha wrote in the only medium hers gave her and it sits in a bag on a table in winter. The difference between dissolution and survival is sixty-six million years and a flood-surge and the blind indifference of a planet that recycles its own crust. The lost record was not worse. It was earlier. Liss was the earliest keeper there has ever been, and so almost nothing of her is kept, and what is kept we made up, with love, and confessed.

You hold both. Liss kept her record and lost it and never knew it was lost, because there was no after in which she could know. Asha kept hers and handed it to Téo and does not know whether the handing will matter, because the after has not happened yet. Neither woman ever held both records at once. Neither one ever can. Liss is in the bed. Asha is in the room. The only place where the count of the day in a hand that is gone and the *there were four* in a hand that is not gone yet can be read by one mind is the one you are standing in right now.

◆

Liss did not write the crier down, the last of its stretch, fishing the empty shoal, because the crier was hers, the one true thing she kept that was only her own and not the marsh's. There cannot be a record of the crier. She chose for it not to exist, out of the same fidelity that made her keep everything else—the dignity of letting one loved thing go unkept. We know about it only because we invented her, and gave her the choice, and the choice is invention all the way down. Most of what was ever kept by anyone was kept that way, in one mind, for one watching, and went out of the world when the watcher did. Survival is the freak.

Asha will not publish the names either. The names are the crier. She has put them into the one other living hand willing to hold them, and otherwise will let them go out of the world when she does. The logbook will not survive everything. Nothing does. It is why she handed it on. A record handed on is the only kind that has a chance, and it is not a good chance, and she took it anyway, because taking the unprovable chance is the thing she finally learned to do.

◆

So we will leave the two of them at the rail and the table, which are the same place.

Liss is at the seaward rail in the last of the river-light with the keeping-book open to the count of the day, and she does not know the surge is coming, and she will never know it took the book, and the book is going to become the centimeter of clay a woman not yet born by sixty-six million years will spend eleven years standing on without knowing whose home it was. Asha is at the table in the winter building with the logbook just gone into Téo's hand, and she does not know how the room came out, and neither do we, and we will not tell you even the part we could guess, because the not-knowing is the whole of the warning.

Between them is a centimeter of clay. One record is lost and one is kept and the difference is not in the records.

It is in the reader.

We have brought you as far as we can. There is one more thing to say, and it is not ours; it belongs to the line in the rock, which has been waiting the whole time for someone to decide whether it is a tombstone or a letter.

You are the someone. You always were. There was never anyone else; that is what the two records are for.

Turn the page. It is the last of it. It is addressed to you.
