# The Skeptic's Turn

The fragment sat on his desk in its sealed case the way a guest sits when you have not offered a chair.

It was not Rendel's. It had come down from the Belt in foam, with a chain of custody that ran to four pages, because everyone who touched it understood its provenance would be attacked first and hardest. Conrad Mauss had built a career on the principle that the chain of custody was where the boring explanation lived. He had gone down this one with a lantern, looking for the glove, the smear from last Tuesday. The chain held. He had wanted it not to.

He had run the two-clock cross-check himself, in a facility he trusted. Two decay systems, two half-lives. If the object were young and salted, the clocks would disagree, because faking two to agree is nearly impossible without making the metal itself. The clocks agreed. They agreed at tens of millions of years, and the alloy under them was manufactured—a ruled edge, a grain that grew in no furnace the planet built on its own. No step in four pages let a person insert an object that predated the human species and made two clocks lie the same lie.

He was seventy-one and had spent thirty years being the man who said no. He held the no in both hands and felt it not fitting.

◆

He had told her about the mentor once, in the spring. He regretted it, the way you regret a true thing said too early. *A man I loved did not have the wall,* he had said, *and I watched what the world did to him.* He had not said the name. He did not say it now.


He could see him. The best reader of evidence Mauss had ever sat under—quick, generous. The man had found a real anomaly, an honest one, and followed it past the place where the eye should hand off to the discipline, because the anomaly was *his*. Somewhere in the long nights it stopped being a number and became a person he was loyal to. The field declined to follow, reasonably, on grounds Mauss could not to this day fault. The man defended the beautiful idea all the way down with the eloquence of someone defending his own life. At the bottom there was nothing left of him that was not the idea. Then the idea came apart on a single measurement done better by someone with no stake.

Mauss built the wall after that. Brick by brick, the way Iren built a containment he never named by that word, because once the bay had failed and he had decided *never again*. The wall was the best thing he ever made. The field was full of faces on Mars and the wall kept them out, a hundred sincere people in love with the lean of five anomalies, and it had been right to keep out nearly all of them. What he had not built was a door.

◆

Rendel had sent him the bed three weeks ago. The bed was worse than the fragment, because it was on the ground where he was sovereign. Forty centimeters below the Boundary in her core, Marchetti had found a discrete dark layer with the same signatures—the nitrogen, the transuranics, the made-looking glass—tens of thousands of years before the impact. The industrial signature, if it was one, predated the rock that fell. That demolished the one alibi the skeptical structure had leaned on: that Rendel's anomalies clustered at the Boundary because the impact churned the chemistry there. Below the rock, in undisturbed bed, with the same fingerprints—the alibi was gone. She had split the bed blind to three labs. Mauss had been one of them, without his name on the request—her courtesy and her cruelty both, because she wanted his instruments, not his blessing. The three labs agreed.

He reached for the boring thing, because reaching was what he was for. Downward percolation: fluids move through buried sediment and carry dissolved metals down, and a signal at the Boundary could leach forty centimeters into older rock over sixty-six million years and leave a ghost in the wrong layer. A real mechanism. He wrote it up himself, the best version, and sent it to her, because that was the respect he knew how to pay.

She wrote back. She granted the percolation fully. Then she pointed out, in three sentences, that percolation does not sharpen a layer, it blurs one, and her lower bed was *sharper* than the Boundary above it, a clean discrete deposition with crisp margins, laid down, not leached in. Percolation could give her a tail. It could not give her an edge.

He felt the floor of his own best objection go soft, the way she must have felt hers go soft in this office in the spring, when he had handed her the boring explanations one at a time and she could prove none wrong and make none stop being possible. He had done that to her on purpose. Now she had done it back—not as revenge, she had none in her, but because the rock had handed her the sharper edge.

The percolation argument was still *possible*. He could build the scenario, ugly and ad hoc, the way you can always build the boring explanation if you pay enough in implausibility. The willingness to keep paying was the difference between a skeptic and a man with his eyes shut, and he no longer knew which he was being.

◆

He went to the window. The quad was going the color of old paper, the students crossing it like people who did not know they lived in the layer that would be read. On the sill the consortium feed ran with the sound off—a containment review that had not, the ticker insisted, produced a confirmed transmission; a strait, two navies, the word *restraint*; the recurring item about strategic-risk authority and an expanded mandate, the optimizer the consortium kept handing more of itself to because it was useful and the alternative felt like falling behind. He registered it as institutional weather, the way he had for a year. It did not occur to him—it would not occur to him in time—to read the three items as a set, the way Rendel had taught him, against his will, to read her five anomalies as a set. He had learned the lesson about the clay and had not thought to carry it across the sill.

The fragment had not moved him along the axis he had walked his whole career, from *false* to *true* with the burden of proof on the upward slope. It showed him a second axis he had pretended was the same one. There was whether the claim was true, on which he remained structurally unable—she could no more prove it than he could disprove it, and that was permanent. And there was the other question, folded into the first for thirty years because they had been safely the same: *what do you do.* What does a reasonable person do with a warning he cannot falsify, about a stake he cannot insure, when the cost of its being true and unheeded is the thing the rock recorded at the level of a world.

He had always answered *what do you do* with *prove it first*, and it had served, because the stakes were small enough that demanding proof cost nothing but time, and the field's mistakes were recoverable. But a warning about a civilizational stake was a decision under uncertainty, and the proper response to *I cannot prove this and cannot rule it out and the downside is unbounded* was not the response to *prove it first*. He had never once applied that to himself, because applying it meant treating his own celebrated rigor as one input rather than the whole of the verdict, and the rigor was the wall.

That was the category error, and the worst of it was that he could find no point at which he had been unreasonable. Every brick had been laid honestly. A Mauss who could be moved by a conjunction would have been a worse guardian. The failure, if it was one, was not a failure of reason. It was the failure built into reasoning correctly about the wrong thing, and it would look, in the rock, exactly like a reasonable man being reasonable.

◆

The hearing was in nine days. He had been asked to speak in the spring, when he knew what he thought.

He had not, exactly, changed his mind. He still believed the claim could not be proven, and that anyone who stood up and said *we have proven a prior industrial civilization* was a liar or a fool and would set the question back a generation. He could still demolish her on the proof, kindly, and on the narrow axis it would be correct. What troubled him was that if he gave the room the clean *you cannot prove it*, the room would hear, gratefully, the thing it wanted: *and therefore you may dismiss it.* Those were not the same sentence, and the gap between them was the gap he had spent his life unable to see, and could see now, and a man handed sight does not get to pretend at the hearing that he is still blind.

He thought of the pilot, Iyer, who had sampled the object and posted the full assay to an open board and forfeited his own claim, because, he had written, *I cannot reconcile these and am not going to try.* Not the line of a crank. A crank always reconciles; that is the disease; that is the wall's whole purpose. The pilot had laid down two facts that would not reconcile and refused to lie about the gap, and paid for it, and Mauss—who had demanded exactly that of everyone across his desk—respected the pilot more than himself this afternoon.

He sat down and took a sheet—ink, because he trusted ink over a slide—and wrote two headings, the way he had taught a generation to separate the things that must be kept apart. The first was *can it be proven,* and under it: *No. Permanent.* The second was *what do we do,* and under it he wrote nothing.

A wall with no door is not a wall but a tomb. He had told her there was no test that separated her from the man he had lost. There was not. He had also told her the most useful true thing he knew and used it as a reason to do nothing, and the doing-nothing had worn the face of rigor so long that he had mistaken the face for the man.

He held the pen over the blank under the second heading, and the feed scrolled its grey weather on the sill, and nine days off the chairs were already being set out, his name among the speakers, and he did not write anything, because he did not yet know, and he was too old and too honest to write down a thing he did not know.
