# Contamination

She ran it again, the eleventh time. The tenth had been the last. She knew the difference between science and the thing you do instead of believing the science, and she did it anyway. The mass spectrometer did not care. It gave her the numbers it had given her at two in the morning and at four and now at the grey end of the night, the Hell Creek badlands going from black to the color of old bone. The numbers said there was something in the Boundary clay of the Rendel core that the falling rock could not have put there.

The iridium was fine. A clean global spike where it belonged, the Alvarez signature, the fingerprint of the day the sky fell. It was the things next to it that would not let her sleep.

A ratio of plutonium isotopes that had no business existing. Not the bare presence of it—fallout from the last century's tests was in the ice and the coral and the teeth of everyone born after 1945. But the ratio was wrong for fallout. It was wrong for anything made in the twentieth century. It was wrong in the direction of decay, of daughters turning into daughters for a length of time that was not decades and was about sixty-six million years.

A nitrogen excursion, the δ15N gone where the carbon cycle does not send it, like something pulling nitrogen out of the air at industrial scale. A carbon excursion bigger than the Deccan volcanism wanted. Spherules everyone agreed were impact glass, and three of them, under the good microscope, with an internal structure that impact glass does not have and manufacture does. She had stopped showing people the three spherules. She could not keep watching their faces change and change back.

Each one alone was deniable. Take any one to Mauss and he would, kindly, give her the out—contamination, diagenesis, a volcanic source, lab error—and he would be right that the out existed, and she could not prove it the wrong out, because the wrong out cannot be disproven. It can only be made to look unlikely. Unlikely is nothing to stand on in front of a man who has decided what the rock is allowed to say.

Only together did they stop being a list and become a sentence. The most extraordinary sentence in the history of her science, built of numbers each of which had an innocent explanation. She had read the old paper from the twenties: if there had been an industrial civilization before us, deep enough in time, we almost certainly could not prove it. The planet recycles its crust. Sixty-six million years grinds a city to a smear. The most you could hope to find was this. Ghosts in the isotopes. A handwriting with no hand. A case that was real and could not be closed.

She had thought it a charming provocation. Not a prophecy about this hour, her thumb over the key that would start a twelfth.

She did not press it. She had promised. She opened the logbook—the paper one, kept by hand because she did not trust herself to a file that could be edited without a mark—and wrote the date and the run number. Then she did the thing she had started three weeks ago and told no one, especially not Téo. Under the numbers, in the small private hand she used for the things not yet allowed to be science, she wrote: *Whoever you were, you were not careless. Careless leaves a mess. This is the most careful thing I have ever read, and you laid it down on purpose, or your death did, and I cannot tell which, and I am beginning to think you couldn't either.*

Then she closed it, because the light was up, and you could not let a graduate student catch you writing to the Cretaceous.

"You didn't sleep," Téo said, when she came out with two coffees and her face arranged into the face of someone who had. He was at the worktable with the core photographs under the rising sun, bright the way a young man is who slept eight hours and believed the day would contain a discovery. She loved him for it and could not afford it.

"I ran it again," she said.

"Asha. That's eleven."

"I know how many it is." She stopped. He was not the thing she was angry at. He was twenty-six and brilliant and had hitched his future to a supervisor about to do something that could end both their careers, and he did not know it yet. "It's the same," she said, gentler. "The plutonium ratio holds. The nitrogen holds. It's been there sixty-six million years waiting for somebody stubborn enough to run it twelve times. No one found it because no one runs a Boundary sample for transuranics."

Téo picked up the close photograph, the centimeter of dark clay with the pale beds above and below, and looked at it the way she had taught him, which was to assume it was lying and find out how.

"Okay," he said. "Tell me everything boring it could be before you tell me what it is."

This was the part that was still science. "Contamination," she said. "Modern plutonium from weapons testing, smeared into the core. The first and best one."

"And the ratio kills it."

"Makes it very unlikely. It looks like old plutonium, decayed, not fresh fallout. But a careful skeptic doesn't have to believe my ratio. If my instrument is mis-measuring a tiny quantity near the detection limit, a weird ratio is exactly what error produces. I can't prove the ratio's real without more sample, and the Boundary's a centimeter. I can't make more of it."

"Diagenesis."

"For the nitrogen and carbon, sure. Burial chemistry pushes isotopes around. I don't think it pushes them this way, but 'I don't think' is not a result."

"The spherules."

She didn't answer. The sun had cleared the ridge and the badlands were turning gold and ordinary. "The spherules I can't explain. And I trust them least, because I want them most. When you want a thing, your eye finds structure in noise. I've stopped looking at them."

Téo set the photograph down. "Then why do you believe it. If every piece has an out. You taught me extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence, and you've got a stack of ordinary-looking anomalies that only mean something if they lean on each other. Why not write 'interesting unresolved anomalies, further work needed,' and keep your job?"

It was the question Mauss would ask, the same blade in Téo's mouth, and the boy's face pulled it out of her.

"Because of the shape," she said. "Each anomaly is deniable. But they're not a random scatter. They're a set. Industrial nitrogen fixation. Transuranics—fission, reactors or bombs. A carbon spike like burning. Glass that looks made. Hand you that set from a layer forty thousand years old and you wouldn't hesitate. You'd say technological civilization, obviously. The only reason it sounds insane is the date. Take the date off and it's the most boring reading in the world. So I have to ask: am I refusing the obvious because the evidence is weak, or because I've decided in advance that we were first?"

Behind him, down the slope, was the dig—the Tanis bonebed, the deathbed that had made her name a decade ago, fish that inhaled the impact debris in the hour it rained down, a whole drowned world in a flood-surge. She had built a career reading that one terrible afternoon. She did not yet understand whose afternoon it was.

"Okay," Téo said, and she heard the click of a young person choosing the harder future, and she needed him, and would hate herself for it a long time. "Then let's make it stronger. Tell me what would make even Mauss flinch. Not prove it. Just flinch. What's the thing without an out?"

She did not know. That was the honest answer, that morning, at Tanis. She did not yet know the thing without an out was not in the clay at all, that no chemistry on Earth would close the case from the ground, that the only evidence that could not be waved away was turning in the cold between Mars and Jupiter, where a man named Roan Iyer was about to survey an orbit that did not behave.

So she said the smaller thing. "I don't know. But I'm taking the clay to Mauss anyway, because that's the procedure, and the procedure is the only thing between me and being the crank he's going to call me." She picked up her cold coffee. "Run it a twelfth time while I pack. When he asks how many, I want the number to be obscene."

Téo almost smiled. "How many is obscene?"

"Keep going till the answer stops being interesting," she said, "and then do twenty more."

She went to break down the trailer. Behind her the spectrometer started its cycle, the patient breath of an instrument that did not care what it found or who it ruined, and she let her face do, in private, the thing it never did in front of anyone—the thing that, across a centimeter of clay, a man holding down the grey at his throat would have known at once, and never could.
