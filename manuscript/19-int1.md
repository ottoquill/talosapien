# What We Invented

We said we would mark the seams. Here is the first, while the mortar is still wet.

The eggs are real. We start with what is real, because we are about to take things away from you. There were nests. Not his—we will never have his—but nests of his kind and his time, clutches of four and five and six in the fossil crèches, arranged with a care no reptile gives. We have the brooding postures. The shared incubation. The communal raising is not a guess. So when we put a man at the edge of a clutch with one finger along a shell, the man is invented and the gesture is not. Hold those two facts in separate hands.

The grey at the throat is real enough. We have the feathers from the deep beds. We can read the cells that in living birds carry color and change it. We do not know they flushed grey with grief. We know they had the equipment to flush, and a body that broadcasts its state on its skin ends up unable to lie well. The grey is an inference standing on a fact.

His name is not real. We call him Iren. We took it from the metal in the clay that first told us a fire had burned here. It is a coroner's name, a name from the cause of death. We gave it to him the way you write *Baby Boy Doe* on a stone, and then could not bear it. There is no recovering his name. There was a sound he answered to, made in a register we may not have heard, and it is gone. We did not find Iren. We made him, out of a true death and a true world and the blank where a person was. We made him because the alternative was to write a paper about anomalous transuranics in the Maastrichtian boundary clay and go home. We have written that paper. No one went home different.

◆

You may ask what gives us the right.

It is the same right by which you grieve a stranger in a photograph. You do not know the woman in the old picture was kind. You grant it. We are granting Iren an inside, on one assumption we cannot prove: that a thing which fixed nitrogen, split the atom, crossed to the belt of stones and built turning worlds, and then argued about whether to trust a mind it had made to save it—that such a thing had an inside of the kind we have. Because we have done every one of those things, and we have an inside.

That assumption is the most dangerous one in this book. If they were like us, then we are not reading a history. We are reading a forecast, written in a hand identical to our own, about a danger they were at that moment building. The only difference is that theirs is a centimeter of clay and ours is still open on the table.

We invented a man so you would feel the forecast as a death. We confess it. We would do it again.

For now the weather is fine. A woman sixty-six million years downstream has just decided to run an impossible number a twelfth time.

Both think they have more time than they have. So do you. So do we.
