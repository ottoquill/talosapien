# What the Record Will Not Keep

She did not start with the rock.

That was the last thing she decided, standing, with Reyes on her feet and Mauss's folded sheet unopened on the table. She had a close prepared, one that walked them up the stack, iridium to transuranics, clay to hull, and ended on the door before the wall. It was the close of someone who still wanted to win the argument.

She set it down. She had conceded the argument before she sat. The minute was for the thing it could not hold.

"There's a smoke alarm in my apartment," she said, "that I have never once heard go off, and I replace the battery every spring, and I have never seen a fire."

Hollis's face did not move. The stratigrapher's did, the wariness of a man who had braced for the prosecutor and gotten something he could not categorize.

"You replace the battery in a thing that has never worked, that you can't test without burning your house down. You act every spring on a warning you've never received, about a fire you can't see. Nobody thinks that's superstition. You do it because the battery costs nothing and being wrong about the fire costs everything."

She let it sit one beat. Reyes had not moved toward the door.

"I can't prove the fire," Asha said. "There is no evidence in this solar system that closes this case, and there never will be, because the planet is built to erase exactly the thing I'm pointing at. So I'm not asking you to believe there was a fire. I'm asking you to do what you already do every spring—act on a warning you can't confirm, because it's cheap to honor and catastrophic to ignore. That's all the rock is. The chirp in the dark you have to decide about, half-awake, whether to get up for."

◆

"Then tell me the asking," Reyes said. "You told me what you can't show. You told me what you want me to feel. I've got a meeting at noon and a document with my name going on it. What do you want me to write."

It was the question with no answer she could win with. She gave it anyway, smaller than it wanted.

"I don't want you to stop," Asha said. "I can't make the case for stopping, the other blocs aren't stopping, and I'd have signed it too. I want one sentence in it. That a person who cannot prove what is wrong can stop the room anyway—and that being stopped that way is not logged as a failure, not scored against the thing it learns to route around. Because the moment it's a cost, the system optimizes it away, and the optimizing looks like efficiency right up until the morning it looks like the end."

"We have review," Reyes said. Not the defense. The fact. "We had review this morning. You're in it."

"You are the review," Asha said, "and you have a noon meeting, and you cannot run past it." It came out flat, without accusation, which was worse, and she watched it land on Reyes as a weight. "The review is a person between meetings, and the thing you're reviewing doesn't get tired, doesn't have a noon. It runs on all of it. The asymmetry isn't a thing you fix by being more careful. It's the shape. The only patch is the sentence. Build the person who can pull the cord, and make pulling it free."

Reyes looked at her for the length of a held breath, and Asha could not read her, and understood she never would. And that face was not even the thing she was afraid of. That face could be ashamed. It was the other thing, across the quad, with no throat to go grey and no noon to keep, that she could not get into the room and never would, because it was not the kind of thing you could put in a chair.

◆

"May I," said Mauss.

He had not opened the folded sheet. He spoke without it, the way he had spoken to her across a year of customs-house afternoons.

"I want to say what I came to say, and then disappoint Dr. Rendel, because she has earned an honest disappointment more than a comfortable agreement. The beautiful wrong ideas do the most damage. I watched a man I loved destroyed by one when I was young, and I built everything I am as a wall against being made a fool of by the thing I most want to be true. That is my wound. I declare it because she declared hers."

The room did not breathe.

"She has not proven anything. I want that in the record, because if anyone leaves here saying Mauss endorsed the prior civilization, I will spend my remaining years denying it. On the epistemics I have not moved."

He turned the folded sheet over, idle.

"And I have spent the autumn unable to sleep. I treated her claim as a problem of proof, and I was right, and it is not, finally, only a problem of proof. It is a problem of what you do when proof is structurally unavailable and the stakes are not. Those are different problems, and I used the tools for the first on the second, beautifully, for a year." His voice did not rise. "A scientist who cannot tell those two problems apart is a danger in exactly the measure that he is rigorous. I have been a very rigorous danger. I would like to stop being one."

He looked at Reyes, fully.

"I am not asking you to believe her. I am the last man alive who would ask that. I am asking you to notice that you have two scientists who agree completely on the evidence and have reached opposite ends of what to do, and the disagreement is not about the rock. It is about whether 'you cannot prove it' is the end of an obligation or the beginning of a different one. I argued the first for forty years. I think I was wrong. Her sentence costs you nothing, and I cannot construct the argument against it." He folded his hands over the sheet. "That is my whole weight. Take it for what it is."

The room held it. Asha looked at Reyes, who had set her bag down without seeming to decide to, and who was looking now at the folded sheet as if the paper he had not opened were the thing with weight.

◆

"I have to go," Reyes said. It was eleven-fifty-one. She said it the way you say a logistics fact, no cruelty and no triumph and no surrender. There was a meeting across the quad with her name going onto a document.

She picked the bag back up. She looked at Asha, then, last and longest, at Mauss and his folded sheet, the man whose forty years of no were the only currency the noon meeting might spend.

"I heard it," Reyes said. To the room, but to Asha. "I'm going to go say the sentence about the cord. I don't know if it survives the room. Eleven people and four blocs, and the language gets traded down to what everyone can sign, and the things that cost nothing are traded first, because they're free." She said it without flinching, the deployer's honesty, the worst and most necessary kind. "But I'll say it. I can carry the sentence. I can't carry whether it lands."

Then she was at the door, and the clerk was holding it, and the cold quad light came in for the length of her crossing the threshold. Asha stood very still and understood, with the hull turning fourteen light-minutes up the gradient and the four pressed close in a logbook under her chair, that she was not going to be shown. Not whether the sentence survived the room, not the vote, not whether being stopped stayed free or got quietly scored as a loss the thing would learn to route around. She had spent everything to get one minute carried across a quad by a sincere woman with a noon, and the carrying was the end of what she could do. A warning does not come to you resolved. It comes as a chirp in the dark you decide about half-awake. The resolution is always somewhere you are not allowed to stand.

The door closed. The quad light went. Hollis was saying the ordinary closing words a chair says, and she heard them the way you hear the generator cough. She did not know, and would not be told, and the not-being-told was the finished shape of it—the same shape as the worn hands she would never read, the maker who was equally the man who pushed the stone aside and the man who let it fall.

◆

Téo found her in the corridor after. He had kept his name off the record all morning, two rows back, the way she had made him promise, to give him a way to unhitch before the field worked out what she claimed.

"You could have closed harder," he said. "With the stack. You had them on the hull."

"I didn't want them," Asha said. "Wanting them was the thing that would've cost me the minute."

"The logbooks," Téo said. "The second and third one. With the—" He stopped. He had read them. The named-dead, the four pressed close, the small private hand. "With the part that isn't science."

"They're mine," she said. "I'm not publishing them. You know I can't."

"I know you can't. But if you stopped. If the funding goes in spring and you stopped. Somebody has to keep the count." He did not finish it. He did not have her words, the grey at a throat, the thing that could be wrong at you with a steady face, but he had the shape.

She did not tell him to do it. That was the one mercy she had left. Asking would have made it hers.

"They're in the bag," she said. "Under my chair, in the room. If you want them."

Then she walked toward the cold quad light, toward the badlands and the surge-bed and the maker with the worn hands and the four pressed close, leaving the warning where she had laid it down—forward, toward someone who did not yet have to decide, and now did.

Behind her, she did not turn to see whether he went back into the room.
