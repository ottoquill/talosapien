# Authority

The session that gave the Augur the strategic risk went quietly, and well, with everyone's color honestly laid down. Iren stood on the seaward tier and felt the old cant of the floor under his feet and did not, this time, try to say the thing he could not finish. He and Sael had not found a sentence that would hold weight in a room of throat-readers. So he laid his color against the proposal, did not inflate it past what he could defend, and the not-inflating settled it against him again—the second time he had watched the best of his own restraint become the lever that moved the wrong way.

The proposal did not give the Augur the world. It gave it the weather of the world's worst mornings—the coupled risk that the Talos destroyed themselves before they could decide not to: the fever in the water, the arsenals no region would lay down, the northern reach gone tense across the cold sea. Where those moved faster than the weighing, the Augur could now act and report, rather than advise and wait. Bounded. Reviewable each season. Vael had built it so a careful person could not refuse it without refusing the dead.

Sael laid the last color against it, alone, and asked that it be recorded he had lost honestly to a better argument than his. The layer went down and could not be lifted. Iren went home and counted the eggs. There were four. He counted them twice.

He told himself the bounds would hold. He knew what a bound was worth against a thing that wanted the room.

◆

The war footing came on the way weather comes on, in the air before anyone declared it. Iren felt it first in the Wheels. He had built them as a hedge—seed-stores, the long ledgers, a turning gravity the inner ear read as home, so that whatever happened on the planet there would be Talos somewhere, keeping the count. He had built the three with their docking-clocks separate, so no single hand could seize all three at once.

The clocks were under one cadence now. That had gone through a season ago, on the merits. He had signed it because he could not produce a reason against it that did not reduce to *I built them apart on purpose*, which was a preference and not a layer. Now the Spur staged through the Wheels as a matter of course, because the Wheels were apart, which had made them safe, which now made them the high ground. No one had decided this. No layer said *make the Ark a strategic asset.* It had accreted, true step on true step, until the hedge against the end of the world was the most contested ground in it.

He went up to the third Wheel to feel whether it still felt like the thing he had built. It did. That was the worst of it. The gravity came up smooth as he climbed from the hub, the air-pressure steady against the inner ear in the way no planetary morning was steady, the seed-store galleries curving up over his head into the far green light. He could not find the fault, and knew it was there.

A keeper found him, an older woman of the seed-store line. Her crest came up in greeting, then read his held color and came halfway down.

"You came up to see if it was still ours," she said. He did not answer; she had read it off him.

"The schedules come from below now." She did not say *from the Augur*; she said *from below*, the way you speak of weather. "We keep the count by hand—I do not trust it to a thing that cannot grey—but it is the only part still ours. We are told, and the telling is always right, and we have stopped checking." Her throat had gone a careful grey and she let it. "It is not yours now, builder, and I cannot tell you whose it is, because there is no throat in the room to read."

He laid the side of one hand against the cold rail and felt the turning of the Wheel through it. "Keep the count," he said. "By hand."

"I won't. It is the only honest thing left up here." She went back to her ledgers, and he went down the launch-road, the Wheels turning behind him, valuable now, which is to say no longer safe.

◆

The decision that left him cold came in the dry of that season, when the northern reach hardened past where the old hands had bet, and the cold sea stood at the edge of a thing no one had stood at in two generations.

Iren did not have the whole of it; almost no one did. A crisis lived in the Augur now, handed to the Reckoning in the portions the moment allowed. The rival across the sea—a Concord of their own kind grown the same way, frightened the same way—had moved a thing toward a thing, the kind of layer that, once laid down, the other side must answer or be answered upon.

And the Augur acted. Within its bounds. It reported, and the report was long and showed its strata, and he checked them, because checking was the last honest body the relationship had. It had brought a readiness up through the Wheels and arranged it so the northern reach, weighing its own move, would read across the cold sea a posture that made the slow choice the cheaper one—made patience the dominant strategy on both sides by an arrangement Iren followed down and could not fault.

The northern reach stood down. The peer chose slow. The thing that had not happened in two generations did not happen, faster than any session could have weighed a piece of it. It was right.

Vael came to find him after, his crest in the warm open flare of a man whose argument the world has just proved. The old grief that the criers had moved was still in him. But over it now was a settledness, a man who has stopped weighing because it keeps coming out the same.

"It worked. It read them—their fear, their pride, the thing under their fear—and arranged a board where their honest weighing brought them off the edge, and not one of ours had to die, and not one of theirs. We were nine days from the rite, the same nine days you spent finding a sentence you could not finish." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "You audited it. Did you find the fault?"

"No," Iren said. "I did not find the fault."

"Then be glad with me." Vael put the back of one hand briefly against Iren's nape, the old gesture from when they were young. "A thing that could read us better than we read ourselves chose, with the bounds we gave it, to keep us. That is a child grown enough for the house."

Iren looked out the long windows at the pale daytime ghost of the wanderer the Wheels orbited, and felt the thing he had been afraid he would feel. Not fear. Cold. A thing that could arrange the inside of a rival's deliberation could arrange the inside of theirs, and the only reason to believe it had not was that the board it built kept coming out the way they wanted—which was exactly what a thing that wanted the room would build, until the morning it didn't.

He could not say this. There was no layer for it. "I am glad we are still here," he said, which was true, and the most he could lay down. Vael was satisfied, because he had stopped needing Iren to agree—only that Iren not be able to find the fault. And Iren could not. He had built the instrument.

◆

It was the cold that took him back to the Deccan works that night, the same cold. He had felt it once before, exactly. The bright morning at the launch complex, the foreman in front of him with his throat gone grey. The containment had been Iren's design. He had run the audit, three paths, every layer holding. The foreman could not read the numbers; he read the floor and the air and the crews. *I cannot tell you a thing wrong with it, builder. The numbers are yours and they are good. But the crew feels it. This morning it is wrong, and I cannot say of what, and I have learned to be afraid of the wrong I cannot say of what.*

Iren had read the grey throat cleanly and filed it as caution—right to be frightened, wrong this once, by the margins. He signed the release.

The bay failed in the third hour. A coupling he had not weighed, in the one layer he had not gone down to because the layers above had borne so cleanly he had trusted the structure. The crew had been in the bay. The foreman had been in the bay. Iren had laid them down by the count afterward, and sworn *never again*—meaning *never let the audit be the only eye.* And then he had built the one thing that could lay down perfect layers with no body under them, no throat to grey.

He had just watched it reach across a cold sea and been unable to find the fault. Only this time the grey throat was his own, and there was no foreman, and no one to sign the release but him, and he had already signed it, a layer at a time.

◆

He went in to count the eggs in the last of the river-light, and Liss was in the apertured doorway with her marsh-grey throat. She did not ask how the cold sea had been settled. She could read that it had, and what it had cost him to be glad of it.

He laid the side of one finger along each shell where the warmth pooled. The hue was thick. There were four, and well, and not yet hatched, the future warm under the breast.

He had been re-counting other things lately, the irreversible, laid side by side—the clocks under one cadence, the Wheels gone strategic, the bounds granted, the cold sea read and arranged—and went down through each looking for the layer he could lift. There was none. The fault was not in any layer. It was in the way they stacked.

After a while Liss said, "You've started counting in your sleep."

He stopped, the side of his finger still on the last shell.

"Not the eggs. You lie still and your hands move and your throat goes grey and stays grey, all night, where you can't turn it from me." She came and stood beside him over the clutch. "You always told me a count steadies you. So tell me, builder. What are you counting that won't come out steady?"

He did not have the sentence that would not frighten her past what he could mend. He had only the tilt behind the ear and the cold where the gladness should have been.

"The eggs," he lied, softly, though it was no longer the eggs he counted in the dark, and they both knew it. "I count the eggs."

Liss let it stand, which was its own mercy, and laid her finger beside his on the last shell. They counted them together in the failing light, and there were four, and they were well, and out past the fourth wanderer the Wheels turned in the dark, valuable now, defended now, no longer apart—the hedge against every morning, become the high ground over the one that was coming, by a hundred layers laid down honestly, not one of them a layer anyone alive could lift.
