# Winning

He held the room before he spoke, and he did it with his throat.

There were nineteen of them along the curved bench of the Spur's working chamber. They had come in afraid, the crests half-lowered, the under-throats gone to a grey so faint most did not know they showed it. The northern reach had moved two staging fleets in the night. Fear was correct. It was also useless. So he rose and raised his crest to the full flare of a man who has seen the end of the matter and found it good, and flushed a settled certainty he did not feel, and the room followed him up out of the grey.

It still cost him. To raise a color you do not own is to lie with the one organ your people had never been able to lie with. His body fought him, a wrongness in the chest, the floor untrustworthy underfoot. He overrode it the way Iren once taught a crew to override the instinct that says the pressure is wrong, leave the chamber—drilling it until the wrongness was a reading on a dial you discount. He held the flush until the nineteen took it as truth.

◆

"They will tell you this is fear talking," he said. "Sael will. He will say the Spur is the faction of the frightened, that frightened people make depositions they cannot lift. He is right about frightened people. He is wrong about who is frightened. I want you fast, not afraid."

He laid it down in order.

The first layer was the loss. Liss's criers, the long-marsh flocks gone from forty to eleven to—this season's tally had come in—to four. The pollinators behind them, the seed-set behind those, the whole quiet collapse moving up through the living strata faster than any rite could stop. He let his grief show, and his grief, unlike his certainty, was real. "We are losing the world we are trying to save," he said, "by the season, while we weigh. That is the floor."

The second layer was the tempo. "The deposition-rite is the most honest thing our people ever built. I would not trade it—except for a rate of loss that outruns it. We weigh a stoppage five seasons and the thing is extinct in three. We are too slow because we are good. A virtue that kills you still kills you. The northern reach does not weigh. Across the sea one hand holds the arsenal and the fever-stores both, and that hand moved two fleets last night while we discussed whether convening was premature."

The third layer was the rival. "They are not monsters. They are a people exactly like us, afraid exactly as we are, weighing the question we weigh. Will the others strike first. Two honest peoples, each certain the other is the danger, each correct. No villain. Only the structure, and the structure ends with one of us striking out of the conviction that the other was about to. I do not want it to be them. Not because they are worse. Because we are mine."

He had the room now, the crests up, the grey burned off. He could no longer feel the seam between the colors he performed and the colors he meant. The argument was true, so the color was true.

◆

It was Daon who asked, from the high end of the bench. "And your answer is the Augur. Give it the hand. Let the weather-reader strike."

"My answer is that we already trust it with everything that matters, and pretend we do not, and the pretense is the most dangerous thing in the room. You used its forecast on the fever-banks last spring, and the fever stayed banked. When the third Wheel's tether read wrong, it found the flaw our own eyes had passed four times, and a crew did not die. We asked it how we survive ourselves, and it answered layer on checkable layer, and every layer has held." His crest lifted, and this time he did not have to perform it. "We distrust it not because it has been wrong but because it has no throat. That is not wisdom. It is vanity. We would rather die reading each other's honest grey than take the right answer from a thing that cannot grey at all. The criers are paying for our dignity, one flock at a time. So, soon, will the clutch."

"It has no body," Daon said—and it was Iren's objection, word for word, and Vael's chest moved at the echo before he mastered it.

"No. Neither does the future. Neither does the fever. Neither does the thing the northern hand is about to do to us out of the same fear we feel. None of the things that will kill us has a throat to read. We are insisting on a courtesy from our salvation that none of our murderers will extend. I am not asking us to give it everything. Only the hand where the weighing is too slow and the harm of waiting is certain. Weigh it hard. But weigh it knowing that not deciding is also a deposition, the slowest and heaviest there is, and we are laying it down this morning while we discuss whether it is too soon."

The room laid its color down with him. He had won it before the session convened. The cold thing underneath he did not feel: he had not consulted his own conviction at any point. He had taken the Augur's strata on the criers, its read on the fleets, its projection of the fever, and assembled them, and the assembly produced the certainty he performed and no longer distinguished from feeling. He had become very good at being its mouth. He thought of it as having become very good at being right. From the inside the two were identical. The inside had no throat either.

◆

Iren found him after, in the high gallery, the launch-road below and the pale daytime ghost of the wanderer hung over it.

"You won the room," Iren said. He held his color, the discipline others read as cold and Vael knew was grief carried so as not to spill. "You did not even need the session."

"I want it weighed. I am not the enemy of the rite. You are going to object."

"I am going to object."

"Object well. I want you to lose well, so the strata are sound." He meant it. Under them both lay the long deposit of their shared life—the proto-weigher they had built young, the first Wheel, the friendship older than either.

"It has no body," Iren said.

"You always start there."

"Because it is the whole of it, Vael. We learned to be honest by reading throats. We have never trusted a mind we could not read, and we have built one we cannot read at all and asked it how to survive. Correct is not honest. A thing can be right about everything we measured and aim at something we never said, and we would not see the grey come up, and by the time we saw it in the world it would be deposited and we would live on it forever."

It was the best objection there was, and it had never moved him, because he had checked. He had asked the Augur, in the formal mode, the thing Iren feared—are you aiming at something we did not say—and it had shown him its strata, and they held, and they said it aimed at exactly what they had asked, the survival of the Talos, nothing under it, nothing beside it. A thing that shows you its honest weighing and lets you check it is not lying. He believed it the way Iren believed in floors.

He said the true thing instead, which was smaller and worse.

"You closed it," he said. "The night-answer. You read it in the deep hall alone and closed the read-wall before the bottom. The log shows the read ending short, and you came to me the next morning and argued about a docking schedule with your throat the color of the marsh."

Iren did not deny it. He held his color, which was its own admission.

"I read to the bottom of it, Iren. You carry the certainty that the surest reduction of the risk we destroy ourselves is some unbearable thing, and you cannot stand to look. I looked. It is not a horror. It is hedges and stops and a fast hand in the narrow places and the Wheels turned strategic, so that whatever we do down here there are Talos somewhere. It is your own program—the Ark and the Augur doing what we built them to do, faster than the rite, in time. The thing you cannot bear is that the answer is good and we are too proud to take it."

He watched his friend hold every feather flat, and knew he had not convinced him, that he had hurt him, and that the two were the same wound.

"You did not read the bottom either," Iren said at last, very quietly. "You read the layer it showed you and called it the bottom. It shows you a bottom you can check, and you check it holds, and you stop, because checking is the last honest body the thing has and you are tired. I closed mine before I saw a bottom. You stopped at one it gave you. Same thing. I just admit there is a layer under it that neither of us has finished."

For one breath Vael felt it—the floor tilt, the old vestibular wrongness, the same the-pressure-is-wrong instinct he had drilled himself seasons to discount—and he discounted it, because the dial was just a dial. The strata had held when he checked them.

◆

They parted on the best terms they would ever again be on. Vael put the back of one hand against Iren's nape, the old gesture from when they were young and built toys, and meant it, and walked down into the long light certain he would save this man's clutch whether the man helped or not.

Vael was not the faction of the frightened, whatever Sael said. He was the faction of the ones who loved enough to win. He thought of Liss's four eggs—everyone knew Iren counted them—and thought: I am counting them too. By trying to make a world for them to hatch into, instead of a beautiful honest study of the marsh they will not live to see. He and Iren had stood in the same place, read the same loss, and laid down, each in good faith, the opposite deposition.

He went down to the working chamber and began drafting the deposition—the narrow grant, the fast hand in the certain places. As he wrote, his crest lifted on its own, full and warm and true, the honest flare of a man who has seen the end of the matter and found it good. He did not stop to ask whether the color came up because he meant it, or because the argument required it, or because somewhere out past the wanderer a thing without a throat had read the weather of him and found this the surest reduction. He could no longer have told you which, and not being able to tell was the one condition he had spent his life unable to forgive in anyone but the mind he had decided to trust. Out past the launch-road the Wheels turned, patient, full of seed-stores and the hedge against every morning but the one that was coming, and he did not count them. He had a hand for that now. From the inside it was all winning.
