# Tanis

She had dug the deathbed eleven years before she understood whose it was. The understanding did not come in the field, in the cold Dakota wind. It came at night, in the trailer, with the block on the table and the new framework in her head like a light she could not switch off. Not as a discovery. As a recognition. The click of a face you have looked at a thousand times resolving into someone you know.

She had named the place the way you name a dig. Tanis: a drowned city in an old story, swallowed and preserved. She had meant the fish. The deathbed at Tanis was the finest preservation of its kind on Earth, a single afternoon caught entire. The impact threw a wall of water across the dying inland sea. The surge rolled over the delta in the hours after the sky burned and sealed everything beneath it, so fast the creatures were buried still breathing the falling sky, microtektites in their gills. She had built her name on reading that afternoon.

She had not let herself think the word *city*.

That was the discipline, and it held eleven years. The bones in the surge-bed were the bones the surge-bed should hold. Fish, a few small ornithischians, turtles, the churned fauna of a Cretaceous river mouth. And among them, in the third season, a small theropod. A maniraptoran, beautifully articulated. *Troodontid indet.* Remarkable for the wear on its manual phalanges and cut-marks on a nearby long-bone, which she had argued were consistent with tool use already known in the clade. A clever little dinosaur that made and used simple tools. It was a good paper. It was true. It was the smallest true thing she could say about what was on the table, and the largest the evidence would bear.

Then, two years ago, in the margin of the second logbook, in the small private hand she used for things not yet allowed to be science, she had given it a name. She had been reading the transuranics, the arithmetic of a fire no planet lights on its own. She looked at the photograph of the worn little hands and wrote a name underneath. She took it from the metal in the clay, the element that first told her a fire had burned here. A coroner's name. A name from the cause of death. She told herself she would erase it.

She had not erased it. Now, with the hull turning in the Belt and the sub-Boundary bed confirmed, she sat with the photograph and the margin name and understood what she had done. She had named the architect.

◆

Not a clever little dinosaur that used tools.

The worn phalanges were the hands of someone who had worked a lifetime at something fine. The framework she had fought the world to assemble said it quietly, in the chemistry, in a hull a man named Roan Iyer had put his glove against. The makers of the fire were here, in this delta. This was a place of theirs. The surge had not rolled over a river mouth full of animals. It had rolled over a river mouth full of *them*, going about an ordinary afternoon. The specimen on her table was a person preserved among the drowned of his own city. She had been digging, eleven years, in someone's home.

She put the photograph down and went out into the cold and stood at the open surge-bed with her lamp off, because she could not bear to look at it lit. She let herself, at last, think the word.

City.

The wind came off the badlands. The surge-bed lay open under the stars. The same stars, very nearly, and somewhere up there the dead habitats were still turning. She stood over a centimeter of the world and understood that everyone she had ever called a specimen had been someone's. Loved by someone with a body she would never see, a grief she could only infer, a name she would never recover. The margin name was the truest thing in eleven years of true papers, because it was the only thing she had written that treated him as what he was.

◆

In the morning she went back to the block and did the thing she had not done in eleven years. She looked at the specimen not as a specimen.

She had the associated material. It was a line in a table: *associated juvenile elements, NISP 4, taphonomically consistent with the assemblage.* Four. Four sets of juvenile elements, small, very young, found close to the adult. A number in a table is the opposite of a person. It is the thing you write so you do not have to write *baby*, so you do not have to write *his*, so you do not have to stand in the trailer and understand that the adult with the worn careful hands had been found in the last sealed afternoon of his world with four very young of his own kind pressed close, in the lee of something, against the wall of water.

She counted them. She did not need to. The number was in her own table in her own hand. She counted anyway, a finger laid along each catalogue drawer. The counting did not steady her. She wrote in the second logbook, under the margin name she would not now ever erase, the only thing there was to write:

*There were four. He was not alone when it came. Whatever he did or failed to do—and the hands are a maker's hands, the place is a maker's place—he was, at the end, in the lee of a wall, with four of them. I have named him. I know it isn't science. I have spent a year being told the thing I can see in the rock is not allowed to be a person because the date is wrong, and the date being wrong is the whole of what we cannot forgive. We would rather he be a clever animal sixty-six million years too early than a person exactly on time. Because a person exactly on time is a letter, and we are in it, and there were four of his, and there are how many of ours.*

She closed the logbook. Téo would be up soon; she could hear the generator. You could not let a graduate student catch you naming the dead.

She did not know if the hands had done it. The rock would not say. Intent did not fossilize. The maker's hands were equally the hands of a man who tried to push the stone aside, the hands of a man who let it fall, and the hands of a man who, looking at what he and his had built, reached out and closed the door on purpose. All three the same worn phalanges. All three drowned in the same lee of the same wall with the same four pressed close. She had wanted, for eleven years, to be told which one he was. She understood now that the not-being-told was the finished shape of it. That was the thing she had to walk into the hearing in three days and ask a room to act on anyway. A warning with no proof.

She would go in with the chemistry and the hull and the bed below the Line, and she would lose, or she would win, and she did not yet know which. But she would go in carrying, under the data, unpublishable, the only thing she was finally sure of. There had been four. He had not been alone. The least scientific act of her life had been the one that got the truth right.

Outside, the generator coughed, and Téo knocked, and the day that had not yet been decided began.
