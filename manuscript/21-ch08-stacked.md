# Stacked

The board took up the long wall of the field lab. She had built it the way you build anything you do not trust yourself to hold in your head. Out where it could not lie to her by being convenient.

Five strips of magnetic tape. Five lanes, each labeled in her own block capitals because the printer's font made everything look settled, and she did not want anything to look settled. IRIDIUM, the spike full and clean, a green dot beside it. The only green dot on the wall. The iridium was the one thing no one would fight her about. ISOTOPES, the transuranic ratios, decaying in the dark since before there were primates. NITROGEN, the δ15N excursion, the air pulled apart at a scale the carbon cycle had no mechanism for. CARBON, the δ13C, the burn, bigger than the Deccan would pay for. GLASS, last, the spherules, no dot beside it. She did not trust the glass.

One vertical red line through all five lanes at the same depth. The centimeter of clay. That was the whole argument. Not any lane. The line through the lanes.

"It's a chord," Téo said behind her. He had come in with the cores from the cold store, the trays balanced up his forearm the way she had taught him. Never one hand. "Not the notes. The chord. Volcano plays carbon. Fallout plays plutonium. Burial chemistry plays nitrogen if you squint. None of them's a song. It's only a song if they're all sounding at once, on the beat. Same depth. That's not contamination. Contamination doesn't keep time."

She looked at him, this twenty-six-year-old who had slept and was right.

"Say that to Mauss and he'll tell you the beat is the impact. A rock fell on the planet. Everything's synchronous at the Boundary. He'll grant me the synchrony and take the meaning."

◆

She had spent the decade since Tanis made her name learning to argue with a man who was not in the room. She carried a Mauss, fairer than the real one and more relentless, because she built him out of her worst nights. This spring he was getting harder to beat. That was the surest sign she was onto something.

His trick was the single line. Defend one lane alone, stripped of the others, and it fell. The plutonium, near your detection limit, a few atoms, and a few atoms can lie about their parentage. The nitrogen, diagenesis, you can't rule it. The carbon, the Deccan, the largest volcanism in two hundred million years. The glass, clouds. Right. Right. Right, alone.

But the outs did not stack. Four different doors. To walk an honest skeptic through all four you had to ask him to believe four unrelated boring processes had each deposited their separate lies into the same centimeter of clay, at the same instant, in a pattern that read as a sentence and spelled the most parsimonious thing in her science.

A civilization. Industry. Fire. Fission. And then nothing.

"Four outs holding hands is a worse extraordinary claim than mine," she said. "But I can't write that. The second I say parsimony, Mauss says priors, and he's not wrong. His prior that we were first does all his work. Mine that we weren't does all of mine. Neither of us can see the back of our own head."

Téo was logging the cores, ticking depths against the drill record. "You keep saying 'we.'"

"What?"

"That we were first. We weren't." He shrugged. "You've stopped saying 'humans.' You say 'we,' like there's an us that includes the date and they're outside it. Trespassing on our calendar." He clicked the pen. "You named them yet?"

She did not answer. She got the logbook.

◆

She kept the real record by hand. Date, sample, run number, result, the corrections crossed out and rewritten so the wrongness stayed visible. A file could be changed and tell you nothing about the changing. You could edit a spreadsheet at three in the morning, nudge a value into the shape you wanted, and the file would forgive you instantly. No scar. No seam. She did not trust the version of herself that worked at three in the morning, so she made that version write in ink, where a change had to cross out what it changed.

The first three weeks of the book were clean science. Then the other handwriting had started, the small private one, crowded into the margins like lichen on a gravestone.

She had named them. She had stopped being able to write "the depositors" and "the source population," because those were words for a process, and what was in the clay was a decision. Many of them. Careful ones. So there was a name now for the one whose chemistry was the most disciplined, the careful one who had laid down the cleanest ratios. Two syllables. A placeholder she had told herself a hundred times she would erase, and had not. And one for the hand behind the nitrogen and the carbon, the slow death of an ecosystem written into the isotopes of the plants, somebody who had watched it and recorded it. She had given the dead names because she could not stand at the place and write number forty-one.

It was the thing the field would use to bury her. *She names them, she writes letters to the Cretaceous.* She uncapped the pen anyway. She had spent a decade once calling a true thing contamination, in another core, and filed it as noise and gone to bed. Filing a warning as noise never felt like cowardice from the inside. It felt like rigor.

She wrote, under the day's runs, in the small hand:

*I keep wanting to tell you that you were right to be afraid of it. I don't know what "it" was. I have the isotopes of your fear and not its name. But you were right. Whatever you built that pulled the air apart and split the atom and then went quiet all at once—you were right to be afraid, and being right did not save you. I'm sorry I can only read you. I'm sorry there's no version where you can read me back.*

It was unforgivable, professionally. It was the truest sentence in either book. She did not cross it out.

"Asha." Téo, from the cores. The other one. "Come look at this."

◆

He had the deep tray out, below the Boundary, the long ordinary Maastrichtian mud where nothing was supposed to happen. A depth marked, the hand lens out, his face gone old.

"Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me it's a smear from the drill. Tell me anything boring." He moved aside.

A thread of the same dark clay—the Boundary signature, the chemical handwriting—at a depth that was not the Boundary. Forty-some centimeters under the line, which at this sedimentation rate was tens of thousands of years before the rock fell.

"The drill could have dragged it," she said.

"Could have. I checked the run notes. Clean penetration, no jamming. And it's not a smear, it's a bed. Its own top and bottom. Laid down and buried, and more laid on top the normal way. If the drill dragged Boundary clay down a half meter we'd see it scored all the way up. We don't. It's just there. Early. I haven't run it. I wanted you to see it first."

She crouched at the tray and let the carried-Mauss work on her, because he was the only thing between her and what she wanted, and what she wanted was so big she could feel her own eye reaching for it.

If the industrial nitrogen, the burn, the fission, was already in the rock before the asteroid, then the impact had not made it. An impact is an instant. It cannot reach down through forty centimeters of older mud and lay its fingerprint into a world still ordinary. If the handwriting was there before the rock fell, then the one out she had granted Mauss for free, the synchrony—

"That's the thing without an out," Téo said quietly. "Not in the line. Under it."

"If it's real," she said. Her voice came out level. "If it's not a drag, or a fracture-fill, or contamination working downward. Téo. This is the moment. This is where I want it so much I'll see whatever I want to see, and so will you, because you want it for me, which is worse. So we try as hard to kill it as we'd try to kill a result we hated. Harder."

"And if it survives," Téo said.

"Then I still can't prove it. I'll have killed the synchrony argument, and Mauss will say downward percolation, fluids move through sediment, a half-meter is nothing over geological time. And he'll be right that it's possible, and I won't be able to prove it didn't. Because I can't make more of the centimeter. Every door I close, there's a door behind it. I could be exactly right about the largest fact anyone has ever been right about, and there is no amount of correct that gets me out of this lab and into the world."

Téo got out a clean spatula and started, with no instruction, isolating the thread, splitting it for three labs so no single instrument and no single pair of hands could be blamed.

She watched him and felt the despair turn over and become something else. Not lighter. Shaped differently. Usable.

If she was going to die certain and uncertified, Cassandra with a mass spectrometer, then proof had been the wrong goal. The clay would never convict. So the question could not be *how do I prove they did it to themselves.* It had to be the other one, the one that frightened her more than being a crank. If she could never prove what the dead had done, could she still make the living afraid of it in time? Of the same chemistry, warmer, in their own air, in the thing they were building now and calling a tide chart and trusting because it kept being right?

That was not science. It was the margin-note thing again, the leap past the evidence she had spent thirty years refusing to make. She could feel herself deciding to make it, the way you feel a layer go down underneath you that everything after will rest on.

"Three labs," Téo said, capping the third vial. "Blind. Coded so even we don't know which fraction's which till the numbers come back."

"Good." She closed the logbook over the sentence she had not crossed out. "When the numbers come back, we're not going to ask whether they prove it." He looked up. "We already know they won't. We're going to ask a different question."

"What question."

"I'll tell you when I can say it without it sounding insane." She picked up the vials. "Pack these for shipping. I want them in the post tonight, before I lose my nerve and run them myself twelve times and ruin them with my own hands."

Outside the light was going down over the badlands, turning the bone-colored ground gold and ordinary again, the way the most haunted places always did, refusing to look like anything but rock.
