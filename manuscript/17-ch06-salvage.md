# Salvage

The smoke alarm went off in the empty cabin at oh-three-forty. That was how Roan learned the heater pump on the number-two radiator loop had died. The alarm was the only thing on the *Margin* that still worked the way it was built to.

There was no smoke. A pump was seizing in a way that warmed a sensor it shouldn't have, and the alarm did its one job, screaming into a cabin with one man in it and a co-pilot's couch empty two years. Roan lay in the webbing three seconds, listening to a thing that was right about something it didn't understand. Then he killed it and went to fix the pump.

That was the job. When something on a forty-year-old hull broke at oh-three-forty, you got up and fixed it, the cold coming through the deck plating into your bare feet. The alternative was to be a frozen man in a drifting can, and the salvage rates on a dead pilot were terrible. The pump was sealed, not field-serviceable. He had serviced it four times. He pulled the dead unit, warmed the bearing until it turned, packed it with the wrong grease because it was the grease he had, and put it back.

You did not go back to sleep after the alarm. The body had its own opinions about alarms now.

◆

Devi would have laughed at the grease.

That was the shape the grief took, two years on. Not the big absences he'd braced for. The small ones that ambushed him sideways. He'd packed a sealed pump with grease meant for a docking collar, and somewhere in him a voice that was not quite a voice said *that's not going to hold, Iyer, and you're going to do it anyway, and write it up so it sounds deliberate,* in Devi's flat Lagos-by-way-of-orbit drawl. He had to stop with one hand on the cold bulkhead and breathe through it.

She had hated the part of the job that was sitting still in the dark waiting for a number, which was most of it. That was why she'd gone outside the day the forward lock failed—a fatigue crack in a component rated for ten times the cycles it had seen. The cabin pressure went in the time it took the alarm to finish its first cry, and Devi had been on the wrong side of it.

Decompression is fast. That was the one mercy. The drills—mask, hatch, valve, brace—were built around the lie that you would have time. If it was the kind of failure that killed you, it killed you before the first step. He ran the drill now, alone, twice a month, and could not have said whether he ran it because it might save him or because Devi had run it beside him a thousand times and the motions were a place she still was.

He flew solo now. Outfits like his were supposed to crew two. He'd filed the variance and paid the higher premium. The math of hiring a stranger had never come out in favor—the debt service, a second mouth, a second air ration. And under the math, the thing he did not look at directly: that he would have to watch a stranger not be her, in the place where she had been.

So: solo. The *Margin*, the dark, and the count.

◆

The dark out here was not empty, which was the thing the planetside people never got.

He talked to Mara most evenings, dispatch for the little outfit he flew under, fourteen light-minutes away. You learned not to ask questions, because a question and its answer were half an hour apart. So you stated the day in declaratives, and somewhere in the lag the two of you built a kind of intimacy out of patience.

"Pump's back up," he told her that morning, the gauge holding steady behind him. "Don't tell the insurer how. Survey's on schedule. It's gravel, Mara. I'll save us both the lag and tell you now. It's very high-resolution gravel."

Her reply came over breakfast, a pouch of something the label optimistically called eggs.

"Gravel pays the note if it's the right gravel. Listen—" and here her voice did the thing it did when she was about to be hopeful at him, "—Okonkwo says the gyrealm money is finally real. This-decade real. They're chartering survey for habitat feedstock now, not just metals. Claims like ours, the boring nickel-iron, the volatiles—that's the stuff you build a wheel out of. Everybody's going to want to live up here in twenty years, in the big spinning cans, and somebody has to find them the rocks. It could be us. The boring part. The part nobody writes the articles about."

Roan ate his eggs and thought about the gyrealms. He'd seen the renderings—the great spun cylinders, green inside, rivers that curved up the walls of the world and over your head. You couldn't keep a species on one rock; one rock was one bad day from over, one impact, one plague, one war. The answer was to put some of the eggs somewhere else.

He believed it, more or less. He was out here, in the thin unromantic edge of it. The renderings never showed the man fixing a sealed pump with the wrong grease at oh-three-forty so the boring nickel-iron could become someone's curving green river. Somebody always did the gravel.

"Maybe it's us," he told the recorder. "Tell Okonkwo I'm thrilled. Tell her to send better eggs."

◆

The orbit he'd been working for six weeks did not behave, and that was the only reason he'd come to this sector.

It had come up as an outlier in the consortium's catalog cross-check, the kind of thing the survey flagged and nobody chased, because chasing it cost delta-v and the flag was almost certainly a bad solution from too few observations. A small body on an orbit that didn't sit right—too eccentric, the inclination wrong, the path a thing got onto by being hit, except the dynamics didn't close on that story. The honest answer was insufficient data, which was also the answer a man told himself when he wanted a reason to chase a thing.

On the fourth day he got close enough for the long-baseline radar to give him a return, and the return was when the survey stopped being boring.

It was too dark. Albedo was a fingerprint, and this thing drank more than carbon should, across a band where Roan had never seen a natural body drink. The shape sat wrong in his gut the way the alarm had. Not round, and not broken-round either, not the cracked-potato shape of a collision shard. It had a long axis that was too long, and a periodicity along it, faint, that his processing kept trying to call noise and kept failing to make go away. Like a corrugation. Like something that had kept the ghost of a structure after the structure was gone.

And it was cold—colder than it should have been for its orbit and composition. He'd taken a spectrum on the closest pass and seen lines he didn't recognize: not the clean signatures of ices and silicates and metals, but something layered under those, trace stuff, in proportions he had no slot in his head for. Then the window closed and the survey clock pulled him to the next target, and he told himself he'd come back to it.

That had been eleven days ago.

◆

He kept meaning to write it up. The protocol was clear. An anomalous body got a flag, the flag got a log, and someone with more instruments and less debt looked at it. That was how a frontier didn't kill more people than it had to. Everybody wrote everything down, so the next person didn't learn it the hard way.

He hadn't. He told himself it was the survey schedule, that one ship's data on a faint return was worthless until he had more passes, that the spectrum was probably a calibration ghost. All of it was true and none of it was why.

The why was that the thing was going to cost him. If he filed it honestly, they laughed and he'd burned credibility he couldn't spare—or they didn't, and they sent someone with real instruments, and the claim stopped being his. You didn't file an anomaly and keep the rock. The one find that wasn't gravel was a find he couldn't afford to give away and couldn't afford to understand.

So it sat in a file. At the end of each shift he opened it, looked at the radar shadow and the lines he had no words for, and told himself tomorrow.

Devi would not have let him do this. *File it, Iyer, you don't sit on a return that drinks light, what if one's on a path that matters.* She always won the ones about other people's safety. Then the lock took her on no one's watch, and the lesson Roan took, the one he was not proud of, was that the universe killed you on a fatigue crack no matter how carefully you wrote things down. So you might as well take care of your own air first.

It was a bad lesson. He knew it. He went to bed with it anyway.

◆

On the twelfth day he did the thing he'd been not doing. Not the filing. But he opened the file, pulled the spectrum up on the good screen, the survey clock paused, in the middle of his shift where he had no excuse, and looked at the lines.

He didn't have the library to identify them. The *Margin*'s reference set told you nickel from iron from olivine from water ice—what a rock was worth—and it was useless for this. He sat with the unlabeled lines, the heavy trace stuff under the ordinary stuff, and had the unwelcome sense of a man looking at handwriting in an alphabet he didn't read: the certainty that it *was* handwriting, that some hand had made the marks, against no idea what they said.

He thought: nothing natural drinks light like that.

He thought: nothing broken keeps a corrugation.

He thought, and this one he put away fastest, the way you cover an alarm at oh-three-forty: it's cold because it's old. Not cold for its orbit. Cold for its age. It's been out here so long the warmth of whatever made it is sixty million years gone.

He had no business with a number like that. It had arrived the way Devi's voice arrived, sideways, more certain than he was. What he felt was not discovery. It was the cold of a competent man who has noticed something he cannot afford to understand, and knows, the way the body knows an alarm, that he is going to have to understand it anyway, and that it is going to cost him everything he came out here to keep.

He closed the file.

"Survey's on schedule," he told the recorder, for Mara. "Gravel's looking like gravel. I've got an outlier I want a few more passes on before I bother anybody. Probably nothing. I'll write it up when it's worth writing up."

He sent it. He sat in the cold cabin with the heater loop ticking along on the wrong grease, holding for now, and he did not run the drill, and he did not open the file again. Outside the hull the thing the dead had built, large enough and far enough away that even sixty-six million years had not ground it down, turned once more, slow, in the dark, keeping the ghost of its shape, waiting for the one man within fourteen light-minutes of it to stop being able to afford to look away.
