# Base Rates

The email came while she was teaching. She read it at the front of an emptying lecture hall, her thumb over the screen the way you cover a wound before you decide to look. The Rendel core program's funding had not been renewed at the anticipated level. The committee looked forward to discussing realignment. Four signatures. One was Mauss's, the only one she trusted to have meant it kindly.

She went down the corridor to find Téo. She had postponed this for a month. The email made it not.

He was at the bench with the new spherule mounts, the look he got when the work was good. She was about to spend it.

"They cut the core program," she said.

He set down the forceps. "How much."

"Enough that I can't pay you past the spring."

◆

She had planned the sentences on the walk over—you should think about Hwang's lab, your transcript is clean, you are not on any of my papers yet—and meant to deliver them as a supervisor and not as a drowning person reaching for the nearest warm thing. She needed him, and need made a liar of her.

"You don't have to decide anything today," she said. That was the first lie.

"Okay," Téo said. He had gone still. He was twenty-six, with a fellowship application due in November and three letters needed, two from people in this building who had just signed a document with the word realignment in it. None of it showed. She had taught him to read rock. He had learned to read her.

"Téo. If you stay, your name gets attached to the Line. Not the iridium. The other thing. The transuranics, the nitrogen, the claim. Somebody on a committee will type your name and mine will come up beside it, with a little invisible flag that says this one worked on the K-Pg civilization thing, and it'll cost you for twenty years. I'm the cautionary tale they tell. I'd be doing you harm to let you walk in blind."

"You're saying I should leave."

"I'm saying you should be allowed to leave. There's a difference. I'm trying hard to be on the right side of it."

He picked up a spherule mount and turned it under the lamp, not looking at it. "Can I ask something that's going to sound like I'm on Mauss's side."

"Please. Somebody should be."

"What are the base rates," Téo said.

She waited.

"That's his whole thing. Not contamination, not diagenesis. Those are retail arguments. The base rate is wholesale. Take every scientist certain they'd overturned the consensus, that the establishment was too blinkered to see. The cold fusion people. The faces-on-Mars people. Then the tiny fraction who turned out to be Alvarez. The lonely right one. A thousand to one. So if you're in front of someone certain and unproven and fighting the whole field, the rational bet is the thousand." He set the mount down. "Including me, if I become you. So tell me why the rate is wrong here. Not why you're special. Why the rate is."

He had loved her enough to use the real blade.

"It's not wrong," she said.

He blinked.

"Mauss is right about the base rate. Sort the whole population of confident-and-unproven-and-against-the-field people, and you bet against almost all of them. Against me. The prior is the immune system, the accumulated wisdom of every science that ever cleaned a crackpot out of its bloodstream. It's supposed to reject me. If it didn't, it would believe everything, and a field that believes everything knows nothing." She pulled out a stool and sat. "That's the honest floor."

"Then—"

"Then here's the mistake. Not about me. About a category. The base rate is the right tool for one question: is this claim true? There the prior is king, and mine is in the gutter, and it belongs there. But that's not the only question. They've collapsed two into one, and the collapse is invisible, and it's killing us."

"Which two."

"Is it true, and what does it cost to be wrong in each direction. Take cold fusion. You're on that committee in 1989. Base rate says reject. What does it cost if you're wrong? You missed free energy for a decade. Recoverable. No one dies of your skepticism. Disbelief is cheap. You can afford it."

"And here it isn't."

"Here, if I'm in the thousand, you wasted your twenties and I burned my career and a foundation spent some money. The kind of wrong that lands on a CV." She had picked up a spherule mount, the glass bead in its resin. "If I'm in the one—if somebody here before us fixed nitrogen and split atoms and built something and then was gone, gone so completely that sixty-six million years filed them as a chemistry anomaly—then it's the only data point we'll ever have on whether a civilization like ours survives the part we're in. And it's a grave. The cost of disbelief there isn't a wasted career. It's that we ignore the one example we've got, because it didn't come with a receipt."

The chiller cycled and settled.

"So the base rate's right about the truth and silent about the stakes," he said, working it out the way she'd taught him. "And the field's treating an asymmetric bet like cold fusion. Like disbelief is free."

"Disbelief is never free. It just looks free, because the cost lands somewhere you're not standing." She made herself say the rest. "But don't let me off. The asymmetry is also what every crank uses. You can't afford to be wrong about my perpetual motion machine, think of the stakes. The stakes are a lever long enough to move any belief. So they only matter if the claim is live. If a serious person looking at the actual numbers feels the floor move. You earn the stakes by first having a case. I think I have one. Mauss thinks I have a yearning wearing a case as a costume. From the inside, those two feel identical. There's no test I can run to tell which I am. I've looked."

◆

She had not meant to tell him about the other core. She told him.

"Ten years ago, before Tanis made my name, I was second author on a sediment study off the Carolina margin. Pliocene. I saw a thing in the trace metals that didn't sit right. A little excursion. Someone senior called it reworking, a hot particle, in the voice that means you are early in your career and this is how you stay in it. I had a postdoc that depended on his letter. I filed it as contamination. I was probably right. Probably." She set the mount in its slot, square. "I never checked. Agreeing was cheap. I called the warning sign in my own data noise because noise kept my career. That's not the only time. I've done it in places that weren't a lab." She did not say Daniel's name. The silence after weren't a lab had a shape, and Téo did not reach into it. "So when you ask why I won't write it up as interesting unresolved anomalies, further work needed, and keep my job—I already know who that woman is. I've been her. She's sensible. She's right almost every time. The one time she's wrong, she's wrong about the only thing that ever mattered, and finds out too late, and has to live in the house with it. I would rather be the crank."

Téo looked at the cut cores stacked along the wall, the centimeter of clay repeated down the array like a held note.

"That's not an argument," he said. "That you'd rather. That's a wound."

"No," she agreed. "It's a wound. I gave you the argument first. The base rate, the asymmetry, the case. Weigh that cold. The wound is just why I can't put it down. The wound's mine."

◆

She told him to take the weekend, to talk to Hwang, who would take him in a heartbeat and never think of the Line again and feed him a career like a smooth road. She'd write the strongest letter of her life with no fingerprint of the K-Pg claim on it. She would not think less of him.

She had braced to need him so badly she'd be ashamed of her relief if he stayed. Instead she stood in the doorway and wanted, separate from her own survival, for him to walk out and be safe. This was what the warning was for. Not for her. For the one standing where she wouldn't be.

She went home and did not sleep. At the grey end of the night she took out the logbook, the paper one, and wrote in the small private hand: Told him the base rate is right. Told him the stakes are asymmetric. Gave him the door and meant it. The argument was always sound. Soundness isn't what makes anyone walk through fire. I can only ever be the smoke alarm. You can't make someone smell the smoke.

◆

He came in Monday before she did. The spherule mounts were already out—all of them, the whole tray, not the three she'd flagged. Beside them in his neat hand a fresh log sheet, a numbered list that began at one and was meant to go past a hundred: a protocol for measuring the internal structure of every bead in the box, blind, scored by two readers. The way you do a thing when you want a number that embarrasses your enemies.

He did not turn around. "I talked to Hwang. She's great. She offered."

"Good. Téo, that's good."

"I told her not yet." He adjusted the lamp. "I worked the asymmetry over the weekend on my own, because I didn't want to be deciding with your wound. It holds when you're not in the room. So." A small sound, the click of something seating. "If we're going to be cranks, we should be the kind they can't dismiss without doing the work. Twenty readers. Blind. I want the number obscene."

She looked at the back of his head and the centimeter repeated down the wall, and she was grateful past speech. Under the gratitude was the shame. She had built him an honest door and still, somewhere she could not reach, had needed him to refuse it. He had. She would never know whether he'd done it because the argument was sound or because she had failed to keep the wound out of the room.

"Twenty," she said. Her voice held. "And then do twenty more."

He almost smiled. Behind it she heard the patient breath of the instrument starting its cycle, indifferent to what it found or who it freed or ruined. She picked up the work, and let her face do, for one unwatched second, the thing she never let it do where anyone could see.
