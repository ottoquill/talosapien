# The Last Spring

The smallest of the four could hold a color now. She spent the morning teaching it to be honest, which was to say teaching it nothing. She sat with it in the apertured light and let it learn from her body that a throat held was a throat lying, and that lying cost more than it bought.

It had begun by holding everything. It would feel a thing—a hunger, a startle, an older sibling taking the warm patch of sand—and clamp the flush down, and she would watch the effort cross the new body like a cramp. So she let her own throat go open to whatever she felt, until the small one's clamp eased and the color came up where it belonged.

There were four. She counted them with the side of one finger and the count came to four. Outside, where she could not see it but could feel it in the vestibule of her ear, the day was settling into the still high weather of the turn of the season, the air level and generous, a day the body wanted to be out in. She had made herself, this spring, note the days that were beautiful, the way other seasons she had noted the flocks that were gone. A record that kept only the losses was a lie of a different kind.

◆

She took the four out to the river. She had not done it before. The crèche kept the young close, and the elders would have weighed it slow. She did not bring it to them. She carried the smallest against her breast and let the three older stumble between her feet, broadcasting their whole hearts into the light, and the city let them pass.

It was a good city. It lay along the slow delta, the low seaward dwellings and the long causeways and the launch-road running back to the dark cliff where the Reckoning held its hall. Above the launch-road, pale in the daylight, hung the ghost of the wanderer the Wheels turned around, so faint you had to know to look. The young did not know to look. They looked at the water and the river-light coming up off the flood in colors she had no word for. She watched them not look at the sky and was glad, and did not let the mind arrive. She had gotten better at that, this spring. She had learned it late, and from grief.

A trader at the causeway-foot was selling the early shoal-fruit, the soft red kind that came only in the days of the turn. She stopped. The trader read her throat—it was open, it was always open—and gave her four, one for each, and would not weigh them against anything. The un-weighed gift still happened in the city. She let the four loud bodies make a ruin of the fruit, and the trader let its own throat go to the warm color of a thing well given. For the length of it there was only the turn of the season and the red fruit and the four.

She knew what she was doing. That is the part the rock cannot give us. We do not know that she knew. We have only the woman we built from a lifetime of her keeping, and the habit says a creature who had spent four seasons noting the beautiful days does not, on the most beautiful one, fail to notice she is hoarding it. So we have decided to believe she knew, and to tell you that we decided.

◆

Sael was at the river too. She had not expected it; the elder did not come down from the hall, and had not in seasons. She saw the old slow crest first, the settled permanent grey at the throat that the whole Reckoning knew. Then she saw the ledgers spread on the warm stone—the old kept-by-hand kind, the kind the Reckoning had stopped keeping when it built the thing that kept without hands. And Sael was not reading them.

That was the thing she carried away and could not put down, after: that Sael had brought the ledgers down to the river and was not reading them. The whole long record of the weighing lay open on the warm stone, and the old one was looking instead at the water. And at the four, who had found the old one the way the young find the oldest body in any gathering. Sael did not move the ledgers out of their reach, and put one old finger out, slow, and let the smallest clamp its three opposed fingers around it.

"You brought them to the water," Sael said. The voice was the deposition cadence, everything weighed, slow as the laying-down of a stratum. "Good. Bring them to the water."

Sael did not say it was unweighed. Sael would have known. She let her open throat say the thing she would not, which was that she had stopped weighing the small things. Sael read it and did not counsel her, which was itself a kind of counsel, from the one creature in the city whose whole life had been the slow honest weighing.

We want to tell you what Sael did then. We have wanted to through the whole of this telling, more than we have wanted to recover anything else in the deep past. We cannot. This is the place the clay goes silent, and we have sworn not to fill the silence with anything but the admission that it is silent. We know the elder existed. We know there was a last act, because everyone has one. The old keep their last choices closest, and the rock keeps nothing closest of all. Somewhere on a warm causeway sixty-six million years gone, an elder of a vanished accounting looked at the water and the four and the open record and did the most important thing the elder ever did, and we will never know what it was. We have decided the honest thing is to stand at the place and say so.

She remembered, after, only that Sael let the smallest hold the old finger a long time, and that when she gathered the four to go on, the elder did not say go well, in the formal mode. Sael said something quieter, and shorter, and we do not have it, and she carried it the rest of the day like a stone, by its weight and not its shape.

◆

Iren came down to the river in the afternoon, which he never did, and that was how she knew.

She did not know what she knew. She read, in the fact of him coming to the water with the work-hall still full of light behind him on the cliff, that something was ending. Not which thing. Not the size of it. She read only the ending, the way the body reads a floor beginning to tilt by an amount too small for anyone but the frightened to feel. She did not ask him what. To ask was to make him hold a color he could not afford to hold.

She chose to spend the day as if it were ordinary, because he had not told her it was not, and the not-telling was the last gift he had to give. The marriage of their two honesties had always been this—she greyed in the open and he held in the dark.

"The smallest holds a color now," she said, when he reached them. She said it the way you say the weather. "I taught it nothing. I sat with it."

He crouched at the edge of the water and the smallest came to him with its whole heart in the light, and he put one finger out and let it clamp on, the way Sael had. At his throat the grey was there under the held color, and he let it be there, in the open, where anyone could read him, the second time in their bond she had seen him allow it. He did not say what was ending. She did not ask.

"I counted them this morning," he said. "From the hall. I couldn't see them. I counted them anyway."

"There were four," she said.

"There were four." He let the smallest keep his finger. "The count from the hall stopped being enough. I needed it to be four where I could put a finger on it." He did not say more, and she heard the bottom under it, and did not press into the silence.

"Then put a finger on it," she said, and he did, one finger laid along each small spine where it lay against the warm stone, four, in the open, on the river. She counted with him, her finger following his from the other end, and the count came to four, and was the only count in the whole city that day that closed.

◆

They walked the four home in the last of the river-light, and she made herself note it. The low seaward dwellings going warm in the falling light. The traders folding the causeway stalls. A single river-crier, far out on the south shoal, fishing the empty water with the folded patient body of a thing that did not know it was the last of its stretch. She did not raise an instrument to it and she did not write it down. It was hers. The smallest, on her breast, lifted its new-honest throat toward the crier's distant shape and held, all on its own, a faint flush of a color of its own, *that, and far, and alive,* the first wholly private signal of a new life, broadcast into a light no after would read.

She noted the day. She set none of it down. There were things you kept by living through them. A record was not the only fidelity and was not even the deepest one. It was the holding itself, faith with what was about to be lost, which was not hope, and which she did not mistake for it.

There were four. The air came level and generous through the seaward apertures, and the wanderer turned somewhere pale and unseen with the Wheels turning around it. The four were loud and well, each now nearly able to hold the smallest flush of a color of their own, and the day had been very fine, and she had kept it the only way it could be kept, which was by spending it.
